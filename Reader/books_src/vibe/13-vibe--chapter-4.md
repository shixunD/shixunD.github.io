## Chapter 4

Absence Makes the Heart Grow Fonder

Rethinking the Rhythm of Communication

True friendship withstands time, distance, and silence.

—Isabel Allende

When Pat DeReamer was 12, her family moved to Indianapolis. As the new kid in school, she was tall and awkward. She felt like a misfit and struggled to make friends. Then she met a girl who lived a few blocks away. Mary Kroger was popular (she even had a coveted soda fountain at home), and she invited Pat into her house and into her life. “Mary kind of picked me up out of the gutter,” Pat says. They enjoyed being Girl Scouts together. They laughed about having to take a sewing class together. They celebrated each other’s birthdays throughout middle and high school. Then Pat went out of state for college.

That was only the beginning of the distance between them. Pat had the itch to see the world—she went on to live in multiple states and travel to 130 different countries. They both started big families: Mary had five kids and Pat had seven. When people are divided by distance and stretched in different directions, they often fall out of touch. Yet Pat and Mary have stayed close for more than 80 years. They’re now in their mid-90s. Their friendship has endured 15 presidents and set a Guinness World Record.

![Photo of two older women, Pat and Mary, standing side by side indoors and smiling at the camera while jointly holding a card or booklet that shows a cartoon character with balloons; one woman on the left is wearing a light blouse and darker jacket, and the woman on the right is wearing a patterned top with a light cardigan, with a framed decorative textile hanging on the wall behind them.](assets/Art25_PatMary_r1.jpg)

It’s widely believed that the key to lasting connection is close and consistent interaction. Ideally, you live on the same street as your favorite family and friends—or at least in the same town. If not, you substitute with daily texts, weekly calls, monthly video chats, and quarterly get-togethers.

That’s not what Pat and Mary have done. Since the late 1940s, their contact has been consistently intermittent. After high school, they didn’t spend regular time together again until the 1990s, when they had vacation homes on the same Florida island. In 2025, when Pat traveled from Kentucky to Indiana to celebrate Mary’s birthday, they hadn’t seen each other in seven years. Their primary touchpoint throughout all the gaps has been a birthday card from 1944, the year they turned 14.

They don’t remember who bought the card or how the tradition started. Every year for eight decades, they’ve sent the same card back and forth. Pat opens it on her birthday, April 1, signs and dates it, and ships it off to Mary, who opens it on May 20 and then mails it back the following year. _Here’s wishing you a BIRTHDAY that really is COLOSSAL_ , the card reads. _Cause it’ll be a long, long time before YOU’RE an old fossil!_ Rereading the card, Mary laughs: “We _are_ old fossils!” It’s the earliest memento of their bond, and it’s become the pebble that keeps them connected.

![Photo of an open, well-worn birthday card showing a large hand-drawn dinosaur skeleton stretching across both pages, with the words “before YOU’RE an old fossil!” written prominently near the top and dozens of handwritten signatures and dates densely covering the surrounding space; on the right page a small cartoon dog is sitting on a stool, also surrounded by signatures.](assets/26_Dinosaur.jpg)

The card lifts their spirits and reminds them to call each other. It’s a hit with their families, who encouraged them to formalize their world record for the longest birthday card exchange. The two friends hope their children will continue the tradition.

When I first learned about the 80-year birthday card, I was touched, but also a bit skeptical. Although the tradition is clearly meaningful to Pat and Mary, does it apply to the rest of us? How much of a difference could sending a card really make? A series of experiments forced me to think again.

![Postcard drawing showing a diagonal striped border, the word “POSTCARD” at the top, and a small stamp box with a checkmark icon in the upper right corner. The left side contains placeholder message text reading “\[Date\] Dear \[former patient’s name\]: It has been some time since you were here at the hospital, and we hope things are going well for you. If you wish to drop us a note, we would be glad to hear from you. \[Staff Member\]”, and the right side has three address lines labeled “\[patient’s address\]”.](assets/27_4c-Postcard.jpg)

It started in San Francisco, with several thousand patients who had been hospitalized for depression or suicide risk. After patients were discharged, staff sent short notes to a random selection of those who declined ongoing care. Over the next two years, that small gesture cut suicide rates in half—from 3.5 percent in the control group down to 1.8 percent. Similar mailings significantly reduced suicide attempts in Iran and Australia.

The notes weren’t elaborate. They featured simple messages of care. During the first year, patients received them every month at first, then every other month. Over the next few years, the notes only came quarterly. But they mattered a great deal. Look at the responses from two of the recipients:

  * You will never know what your little notes mean to me. I always think someone cares about what happens to me, even if my family did kick me out. I am really grateful.

  * You are the most persistent son of a b*tch I’ve ever encountered, so you must really be sincere in your interest in me.




To feel seen, people didn’t need to interact often or have extended correspondence. They were able to stay connected through touchpoints that were significant despite being sporadic and short. That can be true for all of us, in all kinds of relationships. The central factor in sustaining closeness is not the frequency or duration of communication—it’s the depth of connection.

### Goodbye for Now

Many people associate meaningful connection with regular interaction. It’s true that some ties require recurring conversation. If you only talk to your significant other once a month, you’re on the path to having a decreasingly significant other. If you only talk to high school friends and college roommates every five years at reunions, your relationships might become frozen in the past.

Yet daily and weekly catch-ups can quickly become mundane. _I didn’t sleep well. I discovered a hole in my sock. I’m in the market for a new spatula!_ And you’ve probably noticed that in many strong relationships, you can go months or even years without talking and resume as if you’ve never skipped a beat. Old allies slide quickly into their familiar rhythm of riffing on ideas. Close companions jump right into a heart-to-heart about love and the meaning of life. Good pals pick up where they left off, dishing on favorite songs, giggling at inside jokes, and discussing the exasperating conclusion of _Lost_ where nothing was resolved.

What you may not realize is that deep bonds don’t just withstand separation. Distance can actually improve our vibes. Time apart is not only tolerable; it can be desirable. You can see it clearly in romantic couples. In one study, thousands of Americans felt more love for their partners after they’d spent the day apart. When they were out of sight, they started to miss each other—they felt more appreciative (and less annoyed).

Space can serve that purpose too. Surprisingly, couples in long-distance relationships sometimes have stronger ties than those who live together. They tend to have deeper conversations with more self-disclosure and mutual responsiveness. Something similar can happen with siblings when they leave for college: Despite talking less and being farther apart, they often get closer. With space, they’re able to reset the terms of their relationship. Once they’re not borrowing each other’s clothes without asking, they fight less and have more meaningful discussions. When time together is scarce, you want to make the most of it.[[*1]](013_c004_Chapter_4_Absence_Ma.xhtml#footnote-021 "footnote")

Separation doesn’t just help us stay companions and pals. It can strengthen our ties with allies too. Research reveals that the most resourceful sales teams and the most productive remote software teams don’t interact in continuous streams. They alternate between periods of low communication and surges of high communication. In physics, there’s a name for that pattern. It’s called burstiness.

Burstiness exists when an interaction feels fizzy—energy rises and ideas bubble up. It’s like the peak experience in improv jazz: One person plays a note, someone else jumps in to harmonize, and they wind up with a shared sound that no one expected. Burstiness isn’t limited to professional allies—you’ve probably felt it on a great first date, a riveting chat with a pal about a favorite film, or a heart-to-heart with a companion about your future.

Although burstiness is visible when we’re together, it incubates during phases of separation. With time apart, we can explore and learn independently. When we regroup, we have new ideas to revitalize our thinking and fresh energy to rejuvenate our motivation.

### Bursting into Laughter

The most striking case of burstiness I’ve found is the group that’s been called the Beatles of comedy. They were a major source of inspiration to the creators of _The Office_ , _Seinfeld_ , _The Simpsons_ , _South Park_ , _Family Guy_ , _Borat_ , _Austin Powers_ , and countless others. They started building their chemistry in college as a duo and a trio doing comedy shows at rival universities. In their late 20s, the five ended up writing for a TV satire together. Soon they added a sixth member and created their own show filled with irreverent wit and absurd humor.

_Monty Python’s Flying Circus_ revolutionized sketch comedy as a TV format. They paved the way for _Saturday Night Live_ —Lorne Michaels used clips from their show in his pitch to NBC—and had a lasting impact on pop culture. If you call unwanted email “spam,” it’s thanks to Monty Python’s skit about a café where everything on the menu included spam. They even influenced politics: Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher referenced their bit about a customer trying to return a dead bird to a pet shop (“This is an ex-parrot”).

As they made their show, the Monty Python members were together more than they were apart. During production between 1969 and 1974, John Cleese, Eric Idle, Michael Palin, Graham Chapman, Terry Gilliam, and Terry Jones worked six days a week. They regularly pulled all-nighters to finish writing, rehearsing, and filming in time to deliver weekly episodes.

By the time the fourth and final season aired, Cleese had already jumped ship. One of the others called him a traitor, and they were all being courted for other opportunities. It seemed inevitable that they would disband like the Beatles, but they didn’t. Cleese rejoined them on a live tour and a new project on the silver screen. Their performances throughout the 1970s were huge hits, and their first original film—a mockery of King Arthur—debuted to rave reviews. _Monty Python and the Holy Grail_ is now recognized as one of the greatest comedies ever made.

As a longtime Monty Python fan, I was fascinated by the troupe’s ability to maintain their comedic chemistry for well over a decade and extend it across different formats. To get to the bottom of their bond, I combed through their archives and talked with insiders and experts. Their cadence of collaboration crystallized something powerful for me. Although they built their chemistry through an initial phase of intense interaction, they sustained it by intentionally splitting apart and going their separate ways for months. They weren’t breaking up over “creative differences.” They were treating temporary farewells as fertile soil for fresh starts. Their arc reveals that time apart allows us to rethink, refuel, retool, and reconnect.

On a _Holy Grail_ publicity trip to Amsterdam in March 1975, Monty Python decided their second movie should spoof the Bible. At that point, they seemed destined for second album syndrome. They were all distracted by solo projects and budding fame, and they were in danger of running out of material and repeating old gags. That’s when they put their film idea aside for more than a year.

In April 1976, they met up to discuss the project. They found themselves second-guessing their vision. “Are we or are we not going to do a life of Christ?” Palin ruminated in his diary that night. “All feel that we cannot just take the Bible story and parody.”

The first upside of distance was rethinking. Away from the idea and each other, they realized it would be too complicated—and too controversial—to mock the Messiah himself. John Cleese suggested centering a different character, Brian, a bumbling contemporary of Jesus. The room lit up: What if they shot a scene where Brian is renting out a fishing boat, and turns around too late to see Jesus walk on water? They had “enough initial ideas to fill us all with a sort of enthusiasm which has been missing in Python for at least a couple years,” Palin noted. It was peak burstiness. They hadn’t gotten rusty—they were renewed.

It wasn’t until another six months had passed, in fall 1976, that they reconvened for focused work together. They started writing in pairs on a Monday and reassembled on a Friday to read their first original content to one another in several years. “Things haven’t changed,” Palin journaled with a mix of relief and surprise, “in fact…I think they’ve improved.”

They worked for another month until they started to hit a wall in late December. Although some segments earned raves, others bombed—and they still didn’t have an ending for the film. Over their six weeks of bursty moments, various gripes had been simmering. Members got upset when their ideas were rejected and their identities were subordinated to the group. They took another spell of a few months apart, which offered a second advantage: refueling. As researchers Pier Vittorio Mannucci and Lorenzo Bertocchi put it after studying Monty Python’s arc, deliberately disintegrating and reintegrating allowed “a recharging of the emotional and motivational batteries that kept them together.”

Absence makes resentment grow fainter. Going their separate ways enabled Monty Python to let off steam. With distance, frustration waned and enthusiasm gained. Palin later captured the feeling in his journal: “Start of a Python writing fortnight. We tried such a session a year ago and it was not successful. Today, a year later, things feel very different…. without any bickering or grudging…. an amiable chat.”

In March 1977, Monty Python reunited for another burst of joint work. They had collected a series of funny pebbles to share with each other, and in their first meeting they laughed so hard they cried. By the end of the month, they had solved the ending of the film. They owed much of their progress to a third benefit of separation: retooling. They came back with new talents and techniques.

Between the first two Monty Python films, every member worked on projects that stretched their skills. Most notably, John Cleese cowrote and starred in another show, _Fawlty Towers_ , that went on to be voted the greatest British TV series of all time. The discipline of doing a dozen different drafts of each episode transformed him as a writer, helping the group transcend their sketch comedy instincts and deliver a more cohesive narrative.

After a few weeks of collaboration in March, they didn’t resume their group work until July. In another concentrated burst, they reworked the middle of the film and generated quite a bit of new content. They agreed to do the final revisions to the script in early January in Barbados. That was the fourth perk of time away: reconnecting. They became more open to different rituals and wavelengths. Instead of sticking to the way they’d always done it, they were ready to shift their vibe.

Having downtime in paradise together gave Monty Python a chance to toggle from ally to pal mode. In between writing sessions in Barbados, they ate together and watched the sunset together. Some of them went running, swimming, and waterskiing together. They commiserated about the books they were reading and debated whether Shakespeare was funny. They celebrated a birthday and played charades. The spirit of playfulness between writing sessions improved their vibe. “Everyone is warm, comfortable…and therefore amazingly tolerant of any ideas, however devious, deadly or heretical,” Palin reflected. “There’s an all-embracing benevolence in the climate.”

_Monty Python’s Life of Brian_ launched in 1979, four years after they first came up with the concept. It was the highest-grossing British pic of the whole year in America, and in at least one poll, it beat out _American Pie_ , _Austin Powers_ , _Blazing Saddles_ , and _There’s Something About Mary_ as the best comedy movie ever made. Cheers to rethinking, refueling, retooling, and reconnecting.

Sporadic bursts of collaboration elevated the film from saucy spoof to parody with purpose. The title character, Brian, grows up next door to Jesus and gets mistaken for the Messiah:

Brian: You don’t need to follow me—you’re all individuals!

Crowd [in unison]: Yes, we are all individuals!

Half a century later, the message about the dangers of groupthink might be more relevant than ever. In our world of perpetual pings, their rhythm of intermittent interaction is increasingly appealing too.

### Low-Maintenance, High-Meaning

Sustaining connection doesn’t have to be a heavy lift. Low-maintenance relationships get a bad rap. With the right interaction rituals, less effort can bring more connection.

As important as the quantity of time can be for building a vibe, quality time is what sustains it—and nourishes us. According to the data, most people can spend up to 75 percent of their time alone without feeling isolated. The best cure for loneliness is not more interaction; it’s more meaningful interaction.

Meaning lies in experiences that reinforce our ties as pals, allies, or companions. Among romantic partners, spending more time together doesn’t improve relationships. Doing novel and memorable activities does. What predicts feelings of closeness is not the number of vacations couples take—it’s whether those vacations feature shared fun as pals, new challenges as allies, and physical and emotional intimacy as companions.

At work, too, meaningful intermittent interactions are better than daily touchpoints. I’ve spent some time advising the software company Atlassian, and their data show that how often people go to the office has no bearing on their vibe with their teams. What does strengthen team connections is attending quarterly gatherings—people spend three to five days together building their pal bonds over meals and their ally ties making progress on important projects. A single quarterly gathering is low-maintenance, and the right rituals can be enough to fortify relationships for multiple months.[[*2]](013_c004_Chapter_4_Absence_Ma.xhtml#footnote-020 "footnote")

What makes a ritual meaningful varies by wavelength. Whereas allies usually maintain connections by doing shared projects, companions are more likely to opt for conversation. But even with close companions, it’s not enough to get up to speed on the past—relationships are more rewarding when they involve new shared experiences. They don’t require massive amounts of time and energy. An annual weekend together can do more to sustain a bond than a monthly lunch or a quarterly dinner. And on their birthdays, some people solidify their bonds with companions by giving gifts instead of waiting to receive them. That ritual was dreamed up by J. R. R. Tolkien. “To celebrate your life means to celebrate the people who have formed, shaped and touched your life,” a Tolkien scholar explains. “You mark that by giving presents.”

Pals keep their ties strong with low-maintenance adventures, games, and pranks. There are friend groups that stay connected across continents by holding a New Year’s Eve reunion party. My sister and I have an annual Scrabble match on her birthday. In college, my roommates and I stumbled across a corny 1980s book called _Lifeplanning_ , and since graduation we’ve kept in touch by hiding it in unexpected places. Over the years I’ve sneaked it into an envelope of wedding vows and planted it on a bookshelf where it went undetected for years.[[*3]](013_c004_Chapter_4_Absence_Ma.xhtml#footnote-019 "footnote")

Anniversaries are a natural occasion for creating new rituals. I love a tradition created by a book reviewer named Becka Free. A friend told her that once their wedding festivities were finished and they’d opened all the gifts and cards, they were sad—they felt disconnected from their friends and family. At her wedding in 2018, Becka decided to solve that problem. She and her husband gave each guest an envelope with their table number on it. Inside was a blank card for guests to write a letter to the newlyweds. On their first anniversary, Becka and her husband read all ten cards from table 1. Every year since, they’ve opened the cards from the corresponding table number.

On their seventh anniversary, they read a letter from a family member who had since been diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease. It brought them to tears, and one line also made them laugh: “You probably haven’t made out in a while. I insist you do some kissing on the couch.”

The ritual makes each anniversary special for Becka and her husband. It gives them a unique moment together (and helps them remember their anniversary). It also helps them maintain relationships with friends and family who live far away. Becka occasionally sends photos of the cards back to guests after reading them— _remember this?_ —and writes her own notes back. She told me it helps her rekindle relationships with old friends and long-lost cousins. That kind of time capsule is a moving way to get back in touch with people from our past. Our capacity to sustain connections depends on our comfort with reconnecting.

### The Agony and Ecstasy of Reconnecting

We have hundreds or possibly thousands of dormant ties—people we used to know. We’re especially likely to miss our college roommates, favorite teachers and mentors, and childhood friends, since they were part of formative moments in our lives. And we hesitate to reach out to them even when we have a good reason.

In a series of studies, people were as reluctant to contact old friends as they were to write to strangers. After thousands of people drafted texts and emails to reconnect, less than a third ended up sending them. They wanted to…but worried their overtures would be awkward or unwelcome. _Some ties are dormant for a reason!_ Yet most ties aren’t dormant for a good reason. We moved to a new city, changed jobs, started a family—we didn’t mean to fall out of touch. In 90 percent of cases, we end up finding it fun to reconnect.[[*4]](013_c004_Chapter_4_Absence_Ma.xhtml#footnote-018 "footnote")

We put too much pressure on ourselves—and others—to live by arbitrary expectations of frequent contact. As a result, we think it’s harder to maintain relationships than it really is. What ultimately motivates us to reach out is knowing that friendships can survive and even thrive with time apart. That’s what the evidence shows: When we accept that strong bonds can exist without regular interaction, we no longer fret about whether it’s been too long. It turns out that even chimps and bonobos can recognize friends they haven’t seen in decades.

The most basic ritual for reconnecting is wishing someone a happy birthday. But I’ve noticed that it works better on some wavelengths than others. With allies, birthday messages are optional unless you’re close. _If you have a text thread that’s just “happy birthday” back and forth for seven years and you’re not putting in the effort to send a card, it might be time for that tie to become dormant._ It’s unnecessary for associates to send salutations. _As much as I like my dentist, I’d rather not think of him on my day of festivities._ And if a pal misses your birthday, it’s not the end of the world, but if a companion does, it could strike a blow to your relationship. _How could you forget? Do you not care about me at all?_

You don’t have to wait for a birthday. One popular approach is to commemorate a moment—congratulate them on a job or a promotion, or tell them you revisited a place or activity you shared. When I reconnected with a college pal after a decade of distance, I was stunned that she remembered my favorite movie. The longer it’s been since you were in touch, the more powerful a memory flex can be.

A second easy option is to send a pebble that signals _I thought of you_ and _I care about you_. It could be a playlist to make a pal smile, an article to help an ally think, or a reel about bad in-laws to make a companion feel seen. If you want to put in more effort, I have a friend who blocks a Weekly Correspondence Hour in her calendar to check in with the people she misses. And we underestimate how much people appreciate it when we simply reach out to see how they’re doing. Across a series of experiments, when college students wrote short notes to classmates they hadn’t seen in a while (“just saying hi and checking in”), their classmates were more delighted and grateful to hear from them than they expected.

A third starting point is to go right to the heart. _I was thinking about how much you matter to me, and I miss having you in my life._ One December after college, I spent a week writing emails to dozens of people I’d lost contact with, telling them each what I appreciated about them. It didn’t just reconnect us; it reinforced our vibe. More than two decades later some of them still reference how moved they were by what I wrote. Despite our reluctance to do it, saying thanks turns out to be less uncomfortable—and more gratifying—than we anticipate.

Of course, reaching out is a bid for connection, and it isn’t always accepted. People are more enthusiastic about rekindling relationships when you’re on the same wavelength. The longer it’s been, the harder it is to guess what they’ll want. If you try to pal around but they’re only interested in being allies, they might think you’re wasting their time. If you express a desire for companionship to someone who aims to stay associates, you might get a tepid response or none at all.

Even if you’re on the same page about the purpose of reconnecting, it’s helpful to make sure you have the same expectations about the cadence of communication. When waiting for a reply, many people confuse promptness with closeness. How quickly someone answers you is not a sign of how much they care about you. It’s usually a reflection of how much they have on their plate. Delayed replies to emails, texts, and calls are common symptoms of being overwhelmed. Unless a message is urgent, what counts is not the speed of response but the quality of attention.

Most of us default to email or text to reconnect, assuming that a call out of the blue from a long-lost pal would be uncomfortable. Yet when people were randomly assigned to reconnect by phone or video call, their bonds got stronger than if they emailed or texted—and the interaction wasn’t any more awkward. Other studies show that the care hormone oxytocin is released when we hear people’s voices, but not when we read their messages. Personally, I still prefer email, but I’m coming around.[[*5]](013_c004_Chapter_4_Absence_Ma.xhtml#footnote-017 "footnote")

When time is limited, there are still meaningful ways to connect and reconnect. A few years ago, I scheduled my first trip to Australia. I was hoping to reconnect with one of my closest childhood friends, one of my most important collaborators, my first psychology TA from college, a handful of colleagues, and a half dozen former students. I didn’t know how to squeeze them into a whirlwind book tour: I’d be landing in Melbourne on Monday morning and leaving Sydney Thursday afternoon, with back-to-back speeches and interviews in between.

Instead of making a tough call about who to see, I scheduled one dinner in each city and invited everyone. Over the next few months, the updates poured in from different subsets of my Aussie allies and pals. They were hanging out together…without me. One pair who met at the dinner became allies and launched a project together. A trio of women became pals and started getting together monthly for meals and meditation.

The group catch-up began as a necessity of a book tour, but it’s become my staple ritual for reconnecting and staying connected. When I travel, a bunch of people get the burstiness of a dinner and the sparks that grow into flames afterward. I don’t know who will click or what will come from it, but the connections are brimming with possibility (and less exhausting for me than individual meetings). People send recaps after they get together, and I smile every time a pebble arrives.[[*6]](013_c004_Chapter_4_Absence_Ma.xhtml#footnote-016 "footnote")

In the two years since my Australia trip, the three women I introduced have become companions too. When one of them separated from her husband of two decades, another showed up to support her, even booking a massage to help get some of the physical knots out. Recently, the massage booker sent me a photo of the trio together, beaming, at her own wedding.

There’s evidence that introducing others earns their trust and boosts our happiness. Building new bridges between people is even more energizing than deepening their existing bonds—there’s something magical about watching the initial sparks fly. As long as you know them well and they’ve both opted in, introducing potential associates, allies, pals, and companions is much easier than romantic matchmaking, since physical attraction isn’t required. And connecting others keeps us connected to them.

When I invite some dormant ties to a group meal, I make it clear that I’m not adding them out of convenience or as an afterthought. I’m excited to see them and bring some new people into their lives. At the end of a teaching semester, I tell my students that I look forward to staying in touch, but they shouldn’t worry about how often—I’m a fan of staying open to reconnecting.

After _Life of Brian_ , Monty Python did a handful of wildly popular live shows and released their third film in 1983. By that point, their ideas were getting stale, they were struggling to get along, and they were keen to pursue independent opportunities. They made the choice to part ways for good.

Once they split, they didn’t hang out together as a troupe. They’d bonded as allies, not pals or companions. “Most of us are not natural best friends,” John Cleese admitted. “We’re just miles…apart in everything. I don’t think Terry Gilliam has ever said anything that I agreed with.” Eric Idle added, “We didn’t have the slightest interest in each other as people.”

For decades, fans speculated about a reunion, but Monty Python resisted. “I thought we had flogged this horse to death—way past death, in fact,” Cleese said. In 2013, they changed their minds. The five surviving members reunited for a live show in London. They were all in their seventies and aligned on a goal: Don’t embarrass themselves by doing anything humorless or hackneyed.

It had been three decades since they last collaborated as a group, and they felt nervous. “It’s always dangerous when Python gets back together, because we’ve been separate for so long,” Gilliam observed. Yet when they sat down to start testing material, they quickly discovered that they hadn’t lost the vibe—the old burstiness was back. As Idle marveled, “It was like no time had passed.” A filmmaker who attended their first table read noticed it too: “The length of time apart as a group seemed irrelevant, as their chemistry was just so fresh and vibrant.”

The distance paved the way for Monty Python to reimagine their classic sketches and reinvent their act. Since they’d last collaborated, Idle had adapted their first film into a Broadway musical, _Spamalot_ , garnering a Tony and a Grammy. Harnessing his expertise, the troupe delivered a three-hour musical. They sold out a 16,000-seat theater in 43 seconds…and had to add nine more performances in London alone.
