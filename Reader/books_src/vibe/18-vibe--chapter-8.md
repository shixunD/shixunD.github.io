## Chapter 8

Unity Through Division

Crafting Communities Within Communities

A house divided against itself cannot stand.

—Abraham Lincoln

Hormones raged, social anxiety soared, and friendships fell apart. It sounds like a typical week in middle school. But for about 900 seventh and eighth graders on the coast of Southern California, there were much bigger problems.

In 2003, the student community at Goleta Valley Junior High was splintering. Bullying was on the rise. Test scores were sinking. Fistfights were breaking out and students were getting suspended. They’d always had cliques, but there’d been a spike in prejudice between Hispanic and White students. It was so hard to fit in at school that students were turning to local gangs for belonging.

The new principal, Paul Turnbull, had his hands full. He was in his early thirties, and it was his first time running a school. Raised in Canada by a single mother who worked tirelessly to get out of public housing, Paul had to change schools every year of elementary and middle school as they moved. He felt like an outcast every time, and he went on to become a teacher because he wanted to help students belong. Now as principal, he knew he needed to act quickly to unify the Goleta Valley community.

When we confront rifts in communities, our natural instinct is to build bridges. Neighborhoods create associations to strengthen solidarity. Religious institutions host meals and service days to cultivate cohesion. Workplaces design team-building activities and cross-functional collaborations to connect disparate departments.

Paul Turnbull decided to do the opposite with the students at Goleta Valley. Instead of pushing 900 students together, his first move was to pull them apart. At an assembly, he announced his separation strategy: He was turning the school into Hogwarts.

He split Goleta Valley into four houses, with about 230 students each. Just like in _Harry Potter_ , every teacher and staff member belonged to a house too. The excitement was palpable as the students christened each house with a name. After collecting suggestions, they voted in favor of the Red Sea Monkeys, Blue Pirates, White Buccaneers, and Golden Vikings.

Paul recruited an energetic young social studies teacher, Dan Green, to lead the initiative. Dan and his colleagues did everything they could think of to instill house belonging. Students got to pick their house logo, which was emblazoned on everything. They donned house T-shirts, carried house ID cards, and took a house photo. They plastered house posters around campus and waved house flags at events.

The four houses were competing for the entire school year to win the Mariner Cup. Students could score points through individual excellence and citizenship as well as weekly competitions and service activities. Every Tuesday at lunch, each house united to try to win trivia, dodgeball, musical chairs, tug-of-war, apple-bobbing, balloon-popping, and dance contests. Each season, the houses teamed up to collect donations for clothing and book drives. Many teachers used the house affiliations to motivate class participation and collaboration, giving students the chance to crush grammar bingo, history Jeopardy, and science scavenger hunts for their houses. Students started spotting daily opportunities to elevate their houses—asking a thoughtful question in class, picking up trash in the cafeteria, staying quiet in the library.

Two years later, Goleta Valley had a different vibe. Rates of bullying and fighting had dropped. Suspensions were down by nearly 50 percent. And with better interactions came better results. Average test scores climbed by 35 points—a fourfold increase over the prior year. Students and teachers reported that the house system had strengthened their bonds. Teachers watched the community change before their eyes. “Students enthusiastically represent their houses and participate in activities that they might otherwise ignore,” Dan Green noted. “In the process, they build connections and friendships with people outside their normal peer groups.” Instead of one fragmented community, Goleta Valley became four tight-knit subcommunities.

The best way to unite people is not always to combine them into one group. It’s often to divide them into distinct groups with shared goals. The lifeblood of belonging is finding our niche.

### Wouldn’t It Be Niche

A community is a group of people with a common place or purpose. Throughout our lives, we become part of many different communities. At home, we affiliate with religious, recreational, neighborhood, and volunteer groups. In education, we enter school and alumni communities. At work, we join organizations and professional associations. But the fact that we belong to a community doesn’t guarantee that we’ll feel belonging.

In life, we have competing motives to fit in and stand out. We want to affiliate with others, but we also want to differentiate ourselves. We resolve that tension by joining groups that offer a niche—a simultaneous feeling of inclusion and individuality. That niche is called optimal distinctiveness.

We derive a sense of identity from belonging to unique groups. They integrate us with people who are similar to us, while separating us from the norm in our town, school, or workplace. _These are my people, and we’re not like other people._ Different kinds of distinctive groups enable us to vibe on different wavelengths. You can see it when college students rush sororities or fraternities—they’re seeking a community of companions and pals that builds sisterhood or brotherhood around idiosyncratic traditions and values. It’s also visible when animal lovers become PETA or Humane Society volunteers and athletes train for Ironman or CrossFit competitions together—they’re pushing themselves with allies who share uncommon goals. And it’s apparent when motorcycle fans join the Harley Owners Group and Trekkies go to Comic-Con—they’re expressing themselves with pals who stand apart on the road and among nerds.

It’s well-established that we typically identify with qualities and interests that are rare rather than common. We prefer to be part of groups that represent 20 percent rather than 45 percent of a community. That allows us to fit into the group and stand out within the community. Many experiments have shown that people quickly develop affinities for groups that are distinguished by trivial attributes—like having similar reactions to abstract paintings or being the kind of person who overestimates things. Even when people know the group categories are arbitrary, their first instinct is to flock to one. As comedian Mitch Hedberg quipped:

I like my sandwiches with three pieces of bread.

_So do I!_

Well let’s form a club then!

The quest for optimal distinctiveness illuminates why people are attracted to exclusive groups, like Mensa (the high-IQ society) and YPO (Young Presidents’ Organization). They’re not just looking to network with like-minded peers—they’re hoping to belong to a group that shares something rare and confers status. Optimal distinctiveness also sheds light on why it’s so common for groups to carve out a niche within the mainstream. Think of hardcore gamers contrasting themselves with casual players, or vegans distinguishing themselves from vegetarians. The more people fit into a community, the more they want to stand out as part of a subcommunity.

Optimal distinctiveness helped me understand why people tend to identify more strongly with their city than their neighborhood. Cities stand for something—Cambridge is intellectual, Denver is outdoorsy, Paris is romantic, Rio is exuberant, Philly is gritty. Some villages within big cities have their own personalities too; in New York, the East Village is artsy while the Upper East Side is sophisticated. There aren’t many local neighborhoods that offer such clear, singular identities.[[*1]](018_c008_Chapter_8_Unity_Thro.xhtml#footnote-039 "footnote") But good design can solve that.

If I were judging a neighborhood optimal distinctiveness contest, my winner would be a community outside Atlanta. Serenbe is divided into four villages, known as hamlets. To help over 1,000 residents fit in and stand out, each hamlet has its own distinct identity. People gravitate toward the hamlet that represents their individuality. Selborne is the arts and culture hub, with restaurants, shops, art galleries, and an outdoor theater. Grange and Spela are the agricultural and play centers, built around an organic farm and a Victorian park. Mado is the health and wellness mecca, with a gym, spa, inn, and Montessori school. To bring people together in their subcommunity, the hamlets have their own festivals. Mado hosts a street fair with live music, games, and a scavenger hunt.

If you take a stroll through Serenbe, you’ll notice that something is conspicuously missing from the streets. There are no mailboxes in front of the homes. Each hamlet has its own postal pavilion—a central gathering space where people pick up their mail. The pavilions have benches, a community bulletin board, and an events calendar. There’s a running joke that it can take an hour or two to get your mail, because you run into neighbors at the mail house and on the walk there and back.

Imagine if every community subdivided into distinct villages that offered a sense of belonging. In schools, that’s the promise of the Hogwarts model.

### No Place Like House

The house system has a rich history; it dates back centuries in England, where students were divided into houses for sports competitions—and to enhance learning and leadership opportunities. I loved belonging to a house in college, but it never crossed my mind that the system would work in primary or secondary school—and that it could have benefits for adults as well as students. Paul Turnbull understood that intuitively when he became the principal at Goleta Valley Junior High.

I stumbled across Paul’s work while reading research about house systems in schools. On a Sunday morning, I reached him on a video call in Hawaii, where he’s led an independent K–12 school for over a decade. A few minutes into our conversation, I saw his eyes well up with tears.

It broke Paul’s heart to watch students become so disaffected at school that they were joining gangs. He’d seen the house system in some Canadian schools as a kid, and his own kids were reading _Harry Potter_. When the seventh graders voted on their house names, the first question back from his leadership team was “What’s a Sea Monkey?” They wondered if they should encourage the kids to replace it with something real. “Only junior high kids would pick it,” Paul told me. “But that’s the point. We wanted students to feel _I’m part of something special. I’m part of something bigger. And I’m an architect of it_.”

Dividing into subcommunities gave students a niche. As they wore their swag, waved their flag, and made their posters, students became part of a house that was optimally distinct from the other three. Before, seventh and eighth graders had been strangers or distant associates. Now, they had the chance to vibe as pals, allies, and companions as they cheered together, competed together, and hung out at house events together.

My instinct would’ve been to create more than four houses. That way, students could be divided into smaller groups. The reality, though, is that in schools, smaller cohorts don’t bring tighter bonds. In fact, they can make it harder for students to find affinity and acceptance. You can see it in research on classroom bullying.

It seems logical that bigger classes would have more bullying. More kids means more potential targets, more potential bullies, and more people for bullies to hide behind. But in studies of more than 25,000 elementary and middle schoolers, bullying didn’t diminish when there were fewer students in a room. Surprisingly, bullying was _more_ common in smaller classes.[[*2]](018_c008_Chapter_8_Unity_Thro.xhtml#footnote-038 "footnote")

Yes, bullies really do wreak more havoc when there are fewer students. These findings have been replicated in Finland, China, Austria, and the Netherlands. Specifically, classes of 15 to 20 students usually have more bullying than those of 25 to 30. Experts are still trying to figure out why, but preliminary evidence points to two possible factors. In small classes, bullies are more popular—it’s easier for them to wield power over the whole room. And victims are less popular—it’s harder for them to find allies to defend them, pals to hang out with them, and companions to support them.

This research helps to explain why bullying plunged after Goleta Valley introduced the house system. With hundreds of kids in each house, potential targets had immediate allies and strength in numbers. Bullies didn’t just have less power—as they made more friends, they had fewer reasons to bully too. Clinical psychologist Jennifer Greif Green, a leading expert on bullying, told me that the most effective interventions don’t just aim to curb bullying directly—they focus on building community. “The best way to prevent bullying,” she said, “is to connect students to adults who care about them and students who become their friends and protect them as bystanders.” That’s what the Goleta Valley house system did, and Jen would know—after studying it she ended up marrying Dan Green, the social studies teacher who led the effort and wrote the seminal article that put the Sea Monkeys on my radar. Dan told me that as students became happier and more connected to their house communities, they stopped lashing out.

The impact of the houses stretched beyond the school. When I asked Paul about it, a forgotten memory came flooding back. Before the house system was in place, students were often assaulted by gangs after school at the mall down the street. In hopes of giving them a place to escape, Paul and some parents convinced store owners to put the house logos in their windows. If the stores had displayed the Goleta Valley school logo, it might’ve meant nothing to the students. Seeing their house logo was enough to draw them in and keep them safe. They recognized a symbol that represented them.

As schools experiment with house systems, researchers have parachuted in to study them, and the early evidence is encouraging. It might seem like splitting schools into subgroups risks fragmenting students into warring factions, but with good design, the outcome is the opposite. When two Australian schools divided into houses, belonging to a house gave students a stronger sense of belonging to their school. As several public high schools in New York City divided students, teachers, and staff into houses, students reported a stronger sense of community and unique identity when their house was a bigger part of their school experience. After a Catholic school in Phoenix divided into four houses, surveys showed significant increases in perceptions of community. The house system fashioned bridges and bonds across the school. Students reported more connections with peers who weren’t in their classes and with past and future teachers. They gained a sense that schoolwide activities strengthened community—and that they belonged to the school, not just their classroom.

Teachers got a belonging bump too. Three months after organizing into houses, teachers felt less isolated and more connected to the community. They got to know students they hadn’t taught and stayed in better touch with former students who would’ve otherwise become dormant ties. Their interactions with colleagues had largely been limited to faculty meetings and lunch chats. In a house they felt like allies collaborating as a team, and they felt more valued by their peers. The vibe reminds me of color war at summer camp.

### Carving Out a Place

Optimal distinctiveness is not only about belonging to a special community. It’s also about having a special place within that community. People feel both included and individuated when they have a unique role to play.

A classic demonstration took place in Texas. When Austin’s schools began desegregation in the early 1970s, Hispanic students who spoke English as a second language were treated like outsiders by their White classmates. Instead of trying to unite the students all at once, psychologist Elliot Aronson divided them. He split them into groups of five to six with a common goal and unique roles. To prepare for an upcoming test, each student was responsible for teaching one part of a lesson to the others. It was like working on a jigsaw puzzle: They needed a piece of information from every member to put it together.

The jigsaw classroom succeeded in reducing prejudice and building bonds. Having unique roles in a group bolsters belonging; it’s been replicated multiple times.[[*3]](018_c008_Chapter_8_Unity_Thro.xhtml#footnote-037 "footnote") In an experiment across 15 middle schools, cooperative learning activities strengthened connections—and reduced bullying for marginalized students. As students rely on one another as allies, they gain an appreciation of one another’s strengths.

Being needed builds belonging faster than merely being included. That’s not limited to schools; it holds in workplaces too. The best example I’ve seen is at the world’s biggest tomato processing plant, Morning Star. If you eat spaghetti, pizza, or ketchup, you’ve probably had their tomatoes. And they’ve run successfully for several decades without a single boss. To find out how they build a productive community without traditional hierarchy, I went to their San Jose headquarters to study them. They do plenty of idiosyncratic things that wouldn’t fit most organizations. Yet they have a strategy for building optimal distinctiveness that I think every organization should try.

When you join their tomato paste plant, you’re hired to do the work of the person you’re replacing. At the end of your first year, you’re invited to rewrite your job description. For example, a salesperson who loves doing digital art wanted to add graphic design to her role, and a mechanic who cared about developing people aimed to spend part of his time training new hires. To make those dreams a reality, you have to clear two hurdles. One: explain how it will better advance the mission than what you did last year. Two: convince the five to ten people who work closely with you that it’s a good idea. That means you don’t just stand out; you fit in too. The unique job you’ve crafted is connected to the mission, and it plays a distinct role in your team. They’ve agreed that you’re bringing some special sauce.[[*4]](018_c008_Chapter_8_Unity_Thro.xhtml#footnote-036 "footnote")

It sometimes takes a while to find a niche, but we don’t have to wait a year to open the door. With the right rituals, we can build belonging from the very beginning.

### The Early Bird Gets to Belong

Arriving in a new neighborhood, school, or workplace can be nerve-racking. About 85 percent of incoming college students report that they worry about being accepted by other students. And in one poll, over half of respondents found starting a new job more frightening than holding a snake or going skydiving.

It’s normal to feel anxious about fitting in. To reduce that uncertainty, many communities have welcome rituals. Schools plan orientations to help new students start finding their place and their people. Sororities and fraternities hold initiation ceremonies for pledges to take oaths of loyalty and learn secret traditions. Companies run onboarding programs to socialize new hires into the culture. Yet even when they’re well-meaning, these rituals kill the vibe if they’re done in the wrong way at the wrong time.

When you take a new job, the honeymoon often ends the moment you show up at orientation. You get a stack of paperwork from Toby in HR. A compliance manager reads the employee handbook out loud to make sure you know how not to get fired. A leader who’s had one too many sips of company Kool-Aid gives a stump speech. _Congratulations! You just joined the world’s most boring cult._

We can shift the vibe with welcome rituals that help people fit in and stand out. But which is a better onboarding strategy: emphasizing what’s unique about the existing community, or focusing on what’s unique about the individuals joining?

In a pair of experiments led by my colleague Dan Cable, the clear winner was the latter. Highlighting what made the team or the organization special failed to create space for newcomers to express themselves. But when they got to share their personal career highlight reels with their new colleagues, six months later they had higher performance ratings—and were about half as likely to quit. Since their teams recognized their strengths right off the bat, they were able to add value and feel valued. Sharing their unique skills allowed them to fit in by standing out.

There’s no reason why we can’t start carving out that niche on day one—or even earlier. Most companies run exit interviews to find out why people decided to leave. _If only we had a time machine, we could go back to the past and use that information to convince you to stay._ For years, I’ve advised them to reverse that process and run entry interviews. Along with sharing their highlight reels, new hires describe their best and worst boss and their goals for growth and impact. They often report back that the conversation helps them find a niche that they wouldn’t want to leave. And we don’t have to wait until people arrive—my former student Constantinos Coutifaris found that it’s possible to jump-start belonging during the waiting period before people join a new community. Managers “preboarded” newcomers by describing the culture and inviting them to share their highlight reels two weeks before their start dates. Afterward, newcomers understood the culture better, performed better in their first couple months on the job, and were more likely to stay with the company over the next year. Early onboarding was particularly powerful for newcomers who were uncertain about belonging. It signaled that they had a place and a purpose.

Early rituals don’t always help people find their niche right away. That’s a message newcomers benefit from hearing. In education, more than a dozen experiments have shown that it’s possible to boost belonging throughout college simply by having freshmen spend an hour reading letters from older students about how they felt like outsiders before they found their home. It normalizes social struggles as common and temporary, motivating students to reach out rather than withdraw. After Black freshmen read notes from older peers about belonging struggles, they achieved better grades and higher well-being throughout college—and a decade later, they had greater career success, satisfaction, and community involvement and leadership. Knowing that they weren’t alone in feeling alone inspired them to be more proactive in seeking out mentors, joining student groups, and making friends. Reading about their peers overcoming belonging hurdles has also helped first-generation college students find friends and mentors and women in engineering make friends and integrate into male-dominated communities. But the messages only have a real impact if schools create opportunities to connect.

The more physical spaces and social groups available to students in dorms, clubs, and classrooms, the more likely they are to find a community. It’s true at home too: The more parks, playgrounds, cafés, bookstores, and bars nearby, the more people interact with their neighbors and feel that their neighborhood is close-knit. When there are these kinds of third places to gather, it’s easier to vibe with new people and find our niche.

### The Missing Peace

Strong rituals help people fit in and stand out in a group. Yet human history is full of evidence that when different groups interact, they frequently exclude and look down on one another. When that happens, instead of a community, we end up with cliques or tribes. As one of my kids blurted out during a Michigan football game, “Ohio State players are ugly.”

In communities, people feel solidarity with insiders but no animosity toward outsiders. In cliques and tribes, people are antagonistic toward outsiders. Psychologists find that opposition to outgroups is so visceral that even infants show it. Before they’ve learned to talk, nine-month-olds don’t just prefer puppets that help their ingroup—they pick puppets that hurt outgroups too. In that case, they’re favoring puppets that share their preference for graham crackers over green beans. Sadly, they often fail to outgrow that tendency as they mature…as every sports fan knows.

Take the Olympics. The mission is not just to elevate excellence—it’s to promote solidarity between people and peace between countries through a shared love of sports. Yet research reveals that during the Olympics, people actually become more hostile toward other countries. It happened in Beijing in 2008 and again in Rio in 2016. According to this Olympic paradox, elite competition activates national pride and international contempt. But in Paris in 2024, it became clear that those studies were missing something important.

It was my first time at the Olympics. At the basketball semifinals against Serbia, the moment an American player stepped on the court, the arena erupted in unison: “BOOOOOOOOO!” I thought it was general contempt for Team USA. It turned out it was just French fans—they had a specific beef against Joel Embiid, a dual citizen who had betrayed them by playing for us. Although they jeered at him, they celebrated America’s success. When LeBron James passed the ball to Steph Curry, who swished a three-pointer, they roared with delight. In the third quarter down 17 points to Serbia, as we made a miraculous comeback to win, the stadium pulsated with collective effervescence. Even the French fans shouted with joy for Embiid—and even the Serbian fans sitting near us applauded the awesome feat of American athleticism.

I realized that the research on international Olympic hostility has focused on people watching at home on TV, isolated from other countries. Attending the festivities in person was dramatically different. People still wore their country gear and waved their country flags and belted out their country anthems. They were passionately patriotic—they were proud of their countries. They weren’t nationalistic—they weren’t looking down on other countries. Ingroup solidarity doesn’t require outgroup prejudice.

When you attend the Olympics live, there are rituals to help foreigners vibe. Many countries set up public houses, inviting fans from around the world to eat, socialize, and enjoy music together. At events in Paris, announcers encouraged the crowd to cheer for every athlete, and it created esprit de corps across borders. At the pool, after an American and Mexican diver each failed a dive, the whole audience rooted for them to bounce back in the next round. On the track, when a pair of high jumpers faced the crowd and clapped above their heads, 80,000 fans from around the world joined in to pump them up. I understood why the crowds in Paris weren’t hostile toward outgroups like spectators on the couch might be. Our allegiance to our countries was nested within a broader sense of ourselves as part of a global community of sports fans. We were more invested in celebrating greatness than victory.

### Simply the Nest

Attachments to a part and the whole don’t have to be mutually exclusive. Strengthening our connection to a group can enhance our connection to the community, rather than diluting it. Belonging to a subcommunity personalizes the larger community. It makes an abstract, amorphous collection of people feel more intimate and vibrant. Studies demonstrate that when students feel accepted in their classrooms, they have a stronger sense of belonging in their school, and when volunteers engage with local chapters and clubs, they identify more strongly with global nonprofits.

Common interests can unite subcommunities into a larger nest. If you haven’t used Reddit, it’s an online discussion forum where users create topic communities. Some of the biggest communities are for humor, advice, and world news, but there’s also Confession (spill your secrets to strangers) and AmITheA**hole (get honest feedback about whether you’re being a jerk in a conflict). Within each community, engaged users often create subcommunities to explore more specialized topics, like when the Swiftie community spun off TrueSwifties and SwiftlyNeutral channels. It turns out that after participating in these subcommunities, people usually become more active—not less—in the broader community. Whether you’re engaging with obsessed fans or jaded critics, you’re strengthening your tie to a community of potential pals who love to talk Taylor. You fit into your subcommunity, and you stand out in the larger community.

Common goals and unique roles don’t just connect individuals within a group; they link groups together too. When different groups share the same objectives, they’re more likely to vibe as allies—they need each other. That nests them in a larger community. Hundreds of studies have demonstrated that when employees identify with their workgroups, they don’t become alienated from their workplaces. Affinity for their groups and departments intensifies their attachment to their organizations.

I saw it up close when I studied U.S. Air Force officers. Belonging in their squadrons didn’t detract from fidelity to their branch or their country—they saw their squadrons as a microcosm of the Air Force. It gave them a niche where they belonged, and they worked hard to distinguish themselves from other squadrons. Different squadrons took pride in their distinctive reputations; some stood out for speed or accuracy, others for grit or selflessness. And once they stepped into my classroom for a joint session with Army leaders, I could see that each branch had a united ethos. When I asked how they were different, an Air Force general called out, “We’re the smart branch!” The whole room laughed as an Army general clapped back: “How smart will you be after we beat you up?” When the conversation turned to a threat from Iran, they were in lockstep about their respective roles in the mission of protecting America.[[*5]](018_c008_Chapter_8_Unity_Thro.xhtml#footnote-035 "footnote") It was clear that their subcommunities made up one community.

Thriving communities are made of strong nests. They come together when subcommunities feel special, not superior. The goal is to highlight how they’re different—not how they’re better. That’s what _Harry Potter_ got wrong.

### Hats Off

At Hogwarts, the Sorting Hat assigns students to houses based on their talents and penchants. The bravest students go to Gryffindor, the smartest to Ravenclaw, the most loyal to Hufflepuff, and the most ambitious to Slytherin. Although that creates strong nests, it doesn’t weave them together. Grouping students based on similarity maximizes unity within houses…but also antipathy between them. They become tribes.[[*6]](018_c008_Chapter_8_Unity_Thro.xhtml#footnote-034 "footnote")

To create unity, it helps to make sure nests are cross-sections of the community. That way, people belong to a group that’s prototypical of the collective. That’s how Paul Turnbull succeeded where Hogwarts failed. Before becoming a school principal, he taught English, and one of his favorite concepts in the poetry unit was metonymy: “The part represents the whole.”

At Goleta Valley, the goal of the house system was to build a community of subcommunities from the beginning. Instead of waiting until students turned up on campus, the Goleta Valley team got a head start with onboarding them into their new houses. Over the summer, all the rising seventh graders got a house welcome letter in the mail. When they showed up for school, they wore their house shirts with pride. “It made a difference right away—they had ownership,” Paul explains. “It’s so fundamental to belonging. To stand out, while also acknowledging that I couldn’t stand out if it wasn’t for this student over here—it’s the golden ticket.”

To nest the houses within a broader school community, Paul and Dan rejected the Sorting Hat model. Instead of grouping similar students into houses, they optimized for variety to minimize cliques. Along with balancing the houses on demographics, they made sure jocks, theater kids, and student government reps were spread out. Students were thrust into houses with peers outside their usual circles. Everywhere they went, they had a piece to contribute to their house jigsaw puzzle. “I used to hate lunch until the house activities started,” one student said. “Now I can participate in fun events, get points for my house, and make new friends instead of being bored.”

To signal unity, all four houses had an ocean theme. To amp up the sense of community, the eighth graders got to join forces across houses to compete against the staff in a kickball match and a talent show. The house representatives took leadership seminars together and coordinated the lunch competitions together. The houses collaborated to arrange the school dance, an international festival, and community service. Students saw that they represented their house, and their house represented their school. They belonged to one larger puzzle.

* * *

![](assets/page_3_2.jpg)

After dreaming up and implementing the house system in 2003, Paul Turnbull and Dan Green both left Goleta Valley by 2006. Over the next two decades, without their passion and commitment, the system faded away. But as other schools have drawn inspiration from their model, the legacy continues.

In 2024 Dan became the principal of a middle school in the Boston area, and in his second year he decided to bring things full circle by launching a house system. The staff and students had no idea he’d done it two decades earlier—he wanted them to build their own vision first.

The week after I connected with Paul, his wife ran into Rico Ochoa, a graduate of the original cohort of Sea Monkeys. Rico still vividly remembers the exhilaration of winning the Mariner Cup and celebrating with root beer floats: “It was a big deal! It was such a good feeling to be involved in the school.”

Belonging is not only about fitting in—it’s also about standing out. That happens when we find our place in a distinct group, which has a distinct purpose in a community. The heart of unity is knowing that our collective puzzle would be incomplete without each piece.
