## Chapter 9

All for One

Getting Squads in Sync

To be trusted is a greater compliment than to be loved.

—George MacDonald

As my host passes me the microphone, I can see my hands shaking. I don’t usually get nervous before speeches anymore, but I’m about to confront an unusually tough crowd. The general manager of the U.S. Women’s National Soccer Team has called me in to training camp to improve their culture. Some of the world’s most accomplished athletes are staring me down, wondering why a psychologist is standing in front of them and whether I’m going to make them sit on a couch.

It’s spring 2020, and the squad has been crushing it—they’re coming off their second straight World Cup championship. Things aren’t as great as they seem, though. They’ve replaced their polarizing coach, and as they gear up for the Olympics, the players are competing for limited roster spots and time on the field. My charge is to help unify them, so I start talking about the importance of building a group of givers rather than takers. When I ask how they can make the shift, not a single hand goes up.

After the speech, I interview the players one by one. I realize their challenge isn’t the selfishness of _me_ over _we._ It’s the bitterness of _us_ vs. _them._

Throughout the conversations, one issue keeps coming up. A few veterans feel that the newbies aren’t paying their dues or showing enough respect. Meanwhile, the junior athletes feel like second-class citizens. “There is definitely a divide,” one confides. “Newer players feel it.” After finishing my interviews, I send the players a report and ask for their anonymous reactions. One comment in particular jumps out:

I was surprised by how honest it was about the division between the older and younger players. I think, as a team, it is a subject we tend to skate around and not address. So, to see it in writing made me nervous because I don’t think we’ve ever had it out in the open like that. With that being said, I think it is obviously a necessary step if we want to better the situation and get the best out of the team.

One veteran says it’s a ticking time bomb. Some of the others are skeptical that the rift is real, though. When I sit down with Megan Rapinoe, the decorated co-captain, she isn’t buying it. “That’s bullsh*t,” she tells me plainly. “Of course younger players have a voice!” She thinks the problem lies with them: They need to toughen up and take more initiative. “If you want the space, step into the space,” she urges. Other veterans tell me straight up that culture is a waste of time. The squad should be focusing on their skills on the field, not their interactions off the field. Some people might want to describe a team as friends or family, one notes, but “that is not the reality of having 25 adults who all work together.”

I actually agreed with them on that last point. I wasn’t going to ask them to sing kumbaya or do trust falls, and I didn’t care whether they felt warm and fuzzy. I just had to show them how a vibe wasn’t what they expected—and why it mattered. What I shared next convinced some of the doubters that I might be able to help them.

Many people assume that to succeed together, we need to like each other. But a massive study of studies—195 of them in total—shows that being fond of our collaborators isn’t a major factor in collective performance. When researchers analyzed the success of more than 12,000 sports and work teams, social cohesion was less important than commitment to the purpose and pride in the team. And across a series of team-building experiments, focusing on relationships between members was less effective than clarifying group objectives.

Team cohesion doesn’t require affection between individuals. A collective vibe emerges when people have an affinity toward the goal and the group. They don’t have to bond _as_ a squad—they just need to bond _to_ the squad.

### Avengers, Assemble

A squad is a group of people assembled for their ability to achieve a mission. Think of a SWAT team, a flight crew, a surgical unit, or DC’s Justice League of superheroes. Each member was handpicked for specialized skills. Their vibe is usually an afterthought. It’s up to them—or their leaders—to figure out how to get in sync.

Many organizations try to help squads bond by tearing down office walls, hosting social events, and planning retreats. Yet the evidence is clear that these efforts tend to fall flat. In experiments at two Fortune 500 companies, after they introduced open workspaces, face-to-face collaboration fell by 70 percent. The cacophony was so overwhelming that people opted to email colleagues who were sitting right near them. It’s time to close the door on open offices.[[*1]](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-046 "footnote") Company parties don’t always deliver the intended benefits either: Instead of connecting across differences, people mostly get closer to those who are similar to them. And many team-building activities smack of forced fun, which doesn’t turn people into pals, and mandatory vulnerability, which alienates them instead of fostering companionship.

Even when we succeed in building personal bonds, they can be a double-edged sword for squads. On the one hand, friends tend to have high levels of trust and commitment, so they help each other more, work harder for each other, and get more done. On the other hand, friends don’t do higher-quality work together on average, because they sometimes put their relationships above their task. I’ve seen it happen many times with friends who launch startups together. They tiptoe around conflict to protect the friendship until the business flops…and the resulting shock waves end up destroying the friendship.

A team doesn’t have to be a bunch of friends. A company is not a family.[[*2]](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-045 "footnote") A squad doesn’t need to be companions or pals; they need to be allies. To vibe, they don’t have to be in agreement—they only need to be in alignment. Agreement is holding similar opinions; alignment is upholding shared goals or ideals. Some of the most interesting evidence on this comes from musical theater.

Long before they made it to Broadway, musicals like _Hamilton_ , _Rent_ , and _Dear Evan Hansen_ got their starts in nonprofit theaters. When squads come together to run a theater, they have to decide on their priorities. They can focus on an artistic vision around promoting creative expression and pushing boundaries, a prosocial vision of involving and educating the community, an entertainment vision of creating shows that audiences love, or a vision of excellence in producing plays that win awards. Which vision do you think is best suited to securing survival?

To find out, researchers surveyed leaders of over 100 nonprofit theaters on their attachment to each vision, and tracked their ticket revenue and income over time. I expected the entertainment and excellence visions to win out—the surest path to sustainability should be to delight customers and critics with great performances. But there wasn’t one purpose that proved more successful than another.

The content of the mission was a red herring. It didn’t really matter which identity they chose. What differentiated thriving from struggling theaters was whether their leaders aligned on their identity. If the people in charge didn’t see eye to eye on the raison d’être, theaters tended to flounder. They needed to get on the same page about their purpose and build a culture around it.

Culture is a codebook for how a squad pursues its purpose. Building a strong culture begins with aligning around core values. To be frank, most corporate values are bullsh*t. They’re empty slogans and cliches plastered on a wall. Values make a difference when they meet three conditions: express the principles we hold dear, translate into specific behaviors, and come with accountability for living them. That’s where I started with the U.S. women’s soccer team.

### From Storming to Norming

After training camp, I sent a survey to all the players inviting them to articulate their shared values in their own words. I made a word cloud of the principles they submitted, sized by frequency of mention:

![Word cloud illustration of the U.S. women’s soccer team’s shared values, with the largest words “accountability,” “respect,” “humility,” “resilience,” “grit,” “confident,” “winning,” “positivity,” “honesty,” “trust,” and “integrity,” surrounded by smaller values including “openness,” “support,” “sacrifice,” “competitiveness,” “growth,” “vulnerability,” “servant,” “selflessness,” “self-improvement,” “excellence,” “wholesome,” “discontent,” “discipline,” “sulk,” and “relentless.”](assets/67_WordCloud_NEW.jpg)

I analyzed whether the values differed between veterans and newcomers. They didn’t. The two groups largely wanted the same things…but too many things. My colleague Drew Carton has found that a compelling purpose only unifies people if it’s paired with a small number of values. When groups have more than four guiding principles, collaboration suffers—and so does performance. The longer the list of values, the less likely people are to remember them, see them the same way, and prioritize them.

For the U.S. women’s team, the proposed principles were also too bland. It felt like they were playing buzzword bingo. In my field, we look for values to be distinctive, central, and enduring. They aren’t drafted using a thesaurus; they’re crafted around narratives. For years, I’ve taught my students to understand teams and workplaces by asking people to tell stories about their cultures. _What’s something that happens here but wouldn’t anywhere else? When did you feel most proud of your team? What does it take to get fired?_

When I invited the soccer team to share those anecdotes, their principles suddenly came to life. I heard about highlight moments when athletes bled for their squad and lowlight moments when they embarrassed their teammates in public. I challenged the players to identify the four themes that were most vital to achieving their mission and anchor their principles on those stories. Since values are supposed to help us make hard choices between competing priorities, I also asked them to rank their principles. A small group volunteered to identify their top four principles and bring a ranked list back to the team. They rose to the occasion:

  1. Leave it better: Build a legacy for the future.

  2. Bloodbath mentality: Leave nothing on the field.

  3. Do your sh*t: Take ownership over your actions and be accountable for your inactions.

  4. Don’t be an a**hole: Be a respectful teammate.[[*3]](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-044 "footnote")




Under each value, we identified specific behaviors. What does it mean to live each principle? What does it look like to undermine that principle?

_Leave it better_ featured major responsibilities like mentoring young players as well as minor gestures like cleaning up your popcorn kernels in the snack room. _Bloodbath mentality_ was about giving your all—not just in competitions but in practice too. _Do your sh*t_ involved taking care of your body, studying film and stats, and testing your pee. _Yes, this is the first time I’ve seen “pee-testing” on a list of valued behaviors._ It also included making an effort to solve problems, not just complain or sulk.

Their highest value was _don’t be an a**hole_. I told the players I didn’t expect them to be besties or even buddies, but real allies treat each other with dignity as human beings. Some of the younger players felt they hadn’t earned respect—they should stay silent about team dynamics until they’d gained more experience. Several veterans objected; one insisted, “There are no junior players on this team!” They were starting to align around the principle that although some voices might carry more weight than others, everyone had the right to speak up. How else could they get better?

Then I kicked off the dialogue about what it looked like to violate the value of respect. I’d heard that despite several years on the squad together, a veteran had apparently never said a word to one of the younger players. Most of the players hadn’t noticed, and they were appalled to hear it. It wasn’t just demotivating; it was dehumanizing. The team concluded it was unacceptable. I encouraged them to broaden their focus beyond one athlete and establish a standard for the whole squad. _“Acknowledge each other’s existence” is a low bar for an interaction ritual._ They aligned on senior players welcoming junior players rather than ignoring them and taking the time to show appreciation to staff as well as teammates.

Regardless of how teammates feel about each other, how they treat each other matters. Disrespect is corrosive to collaboration. That’s the conclusion of over 400 studies, and some of the most striking demonstrations are in sports teams and medical squads. After NBA players had abusive coaches, they were less effective teammates. Even six years later on a new team, they added less value on the court and got charged with more technical fouls.[[*4]](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-043 "footnote") And after hospital units were briefly insulted by an expert, they were about 15 percent less accurate in their diagnoses and procedures.

Respect is a non-negotiable value for being good allies. But there’s a time and a place for a squad to become pals and companions too.

### Feeling the Love

In the early days of Airbnb, the founders called it a family. They abandoned that language when they realized it was breeding unconditional loyalty, making it hard to hold people accountable for achieving excellence and living their values. As CEO, Brian Chesky started describing the company as a mission-driven organization, like a high-performing sports team. I assumed that meant they’d focus on becoming allies, not pals or companions. Then the pandemic happened.

In spring 2020 when travel came to a halt, Airbnb lost most of its business. In early May, Brian wrote a letter to the company announcing that they were laying off 1,900 employees (about a quarter of the staff). I’ve railed against downsizings for years—the evidence is clear that they’re usually a mistake—but this one was inevitable, and Airbnb took the most compassionate approach I’ve ever seen.[[*5]](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-042 "footnote")

Airbnb launched an alumni talent directory, inviting those who lost their jobs to display their profiles, resumes, and samples of their work. They kept many of their recruiters on staff and turned them into an alumni placement team—instead of looking for people to fill roles at Airbnb, they were charged with helping former colleagues find new roles at other companies. They also offered four months of free career services, a full year of health insurance, and generous severance and equity in the company.

Still, there was one word in Brian’s letter that didn’t sit right with me. It was _love_. “We will have to part with teammates that we love and value,” he wrote. “I have a deep feeling of love for all of you.” How could he love them all? He didn’t even know them all personally! And why did he want love in a company that wasn’t a family?

When I met Brian, I pushed back on it. He acknowledged that their top core value— _champion the mission_ —revolved around being good allies. Then he told me their second value was _be a host._ People are expected to care about their colleagues, treating them like guests in their home. That’s companionship. Airbnb’s two other company values signal pal bonds. They’re _embrace the adventure_ and _be a cereal entrepreneur_ , which is about being as bold and creative as the founders were when they saved their fledging company from bankruptcy by selling politically themed cereal (Cap’n McCain’s and Obama O’s). They have plenty of pal fun.

There’s a case for fostering that kind of team spirit—in the right measure and on the right wavelength. Personal bonds aren’t necessary to build strong squads, but in certain cases they can be beneficial. How much emphasis to put on them depends on the nature of the squad and their mission.

Early in my career, I studied what motivates firefighters. It was clear that the mission of saving lives made them strong allies. As I visited fire stations and rode along with crews, I noticed that they had different vibes—some joked around a lot while others bear-hugged. I didn’t think much about it until my colleague Nancy Rothbard coauthored a study comparing pal and companion cultures among firefighters. In pal squads, rituals revolved around joy and amusement. They joked around to handle grief, defused tension by telling stories about unusual calls and ribbing each other with playful nicknames like Shrek and Horsehead, and pulled pranks to socialize rookies. _Highlights: swapping their baby powder out for flour and convincing them that the only way to wash off rare bacteria was to shower with salt._ Companion squads organized more rituals around fondness and empathy. When their colleagues were dealing with trauma, they responded with compassion and sometimes called in counselors. They showed care with little pebbles, remembering each other’s birthdays and stocking the kitchen with each other’s favorite treats. They even helped one another resolve marital conflicts and raised money for one of their least liked colleagues to save his house.

The data revealed that the two types of squads excelled on contrasting metrics. Pal squads were quicker: They responded more rapidly to calls. Companion squads were safer: They got into fewer accidents. Joy is good for creativity and velocity—it fuels flexible thought and fast action. Care is better for vigilance and reliability—concern makes people cautious.[[*6]](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-041 "footnote")

Culture Tradeoffs in Squads

![Diagram showing three overlapping circles labeled “Companions – you care about each other,” “Allies – you can count on each other,” and “Pals – you like each other.” The Companions–Allies overlap reads “Reliable, but may not be fun or creative,” the Companions–Pals overlap reads “Enjoyable, until visions clash and you flop,” the Allies–Pals overlap reads “Dynamic, but potentially complacent,” and the center where all three intersect reads “Might be a lot of effort, but worth it if you collaborate closely and consistently.”](assets/68.jpg)

The longer a squad is together, the more worthwhile it is for allies to become pals or companions too. There’s extensive evidence that in temporary teams, personal connections aren’t a major priority for performance. When people are rotating in and out of squads or working in shifts—like sports teams and restaurant staff—it’s fine to focus on being allies. Personal bonds become more important in stable squads, where people expect to have more meaningful connections. Since the firefighters worked in crews with consistent members, being pals or companions made them stronger allies.

Airbnb is in a similar position: They build intact teams. Pal bonds make them more innovative and companion connections make them more dependable. They take their four core values so seriously that they’ve built an entire accountability system around them. In performance reviews, employees are evaluated both on what they accomplish and on how they accomplish it. In hiring, job candidates aren’t just vetted on their skills—they’re assessed on their potential to live the values.

In an ideal world, we’d screen out bad apples to prevent them from spoiling the barrel. I have a few favorite ways to spot people who might detract from a squad. I’m told they also come in handy in dating:

  * Look at how they treat the restaurant server and the makeup artist. Selfish takers are great fakers when kissing up to influential people, but they kick down when dealing with those who can’t seemingly do them any good. In interviews, I’ve learned to ask candidates to name a few people whose careers they’ve advanced. It’s a red flag if they name-drop or only mention people with higher power and status.

  * Find out who their role models are. If they respect others primarily based on money, power, fame, or beauty, it’s a clue that they’re more extrinsically than intrinsically motivated. If the people they admire wouldn’t be ideal for your squad, they probably won’t be either.

  * Beware of those who always play the victim. When people are constantly airing their grievances like every day is Festivus, it’s a warning sign of narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and entitlement. When I’m vetting candidates, I like to ask them to explain their setbacks and shortcomings. If they consistently throw blame rather than taking responsibility, proceed with caution.




We don’t always have the luxury of choosing our squad. Sometimes, like the Avengers, they’re picked for us. It might be hard to imagine aligning with people who don’t share our goals—let alone violate our values. But squads can turn adversaries into allies. The transition requires a key ingredient: accountability. And building accountability demands new rituals. To see what I mean, look no further than a video game.

### Mirror, Mirror, in the Brawl

The most popular video game in esports is League of Legends. Over 100 million people play in rotating squads every year, working together to destroy their enemy’s base. It’s famous for epic online battles of friends and strangers—and for toxic behaviors like sexist, racist, and homophobic comments. After years of strife, the game’s creators wanted to see if they could use science to weed out disrespect. They launched some experiments, starting with some basic accountability. When players were reported for bad behavior, other players volunteered to form a tribunal, reviewing and voting on whether to ban them. But when offenders were allowed to return after a ban, they often slipped back into their repugnant rituals.

In the next iteration, the researchers added different kinds of feedback to the accountability. When offenders were kicked out, they received reform cards explaining how they’d violated the community’s code of values. If the card simply listed the category of behavior that got them booted, over the next few months, half of the players stopped being nasty. If the card displayed the specific chat logs that got them banned, 70 percent reformed. And if players got the specific feedback immediately—within five to ten minutes after their offenses—a whopping 92 percent turned over a new leaf.

Disrespect often persists because people rationalize aggression. _I was under a lot of pressure—they shouldn’t take it personally. I had to do something to get them to listen._ They see it as an isolated circumstance, not a reflection of their character. Holding up a mirror jolts them out of self-justification. They can’t deny or deflect; they’re forced to confront their consistent pattern of bad behavior. _Whoa…am I the a**hole?_ It motivates them to take responsibility for their choices and start changing their rituals.

The mirror worked for the players in the game because it provided specific and immediate feedback. It interrupted their old if-then patterns in real time. The team wanted to see if these learnings could translate from the screen to their headquarters. Would the same approach make a difference for employees in the office where League of Legends was made?

The fact that the employees were avid gamers gave the researchers access to tons of data. They were able to track the abusive patterns of their own workforce inside the game. They identified the 30 people with the highest toxicity rates and then scheduled individual meetings with them to review their chat logs. Seeing how they’d violated shared values was a catalyzing moment for becoming better allies. “Pretty much everyone we spoke with was appalled at their own behavior,” one insider said. “We actually received some essays from employees vowing to change their ways and become not just more considerate gamers but better people.”

To create accountability, feedback needs to come with consequences. I once got a call from a law firm that had a major problem with senior partners hazing junior associates. Some lawyers made partner solely by generating revenue and ruled by insult and intimidation. They overloaded newly minted attorneys with pointless tasks, lambasted them for tiny typos, and threatened to fire them if they didn’t work hundred-hour weeks. Partners had no accountability for their behavior.

![Drawing of an office labeled “ATTILA THE MANAGER,” showing a bare-chested, helmeted manager holding a spear and bow while standing at a desk in a doorway, three arrows sticking out of an empty swivel chair in the open-plan workspace, and two employees sitting near computer monitors, with one saying, “YES, BUT HE GETS RESULTS,” and the bottom right corner showing the signature “TOM FISHBURNE” and “marketoonist.com.”](assets/Art69_Attila_r1.jpg)

The young attorneys had little power, and since the firm was a partnership, no one had the authority to fire the worst offenders. I proposed an unusual incentive instead. We had the junior associates independently and anonymously rank the senior partners from best to worst. When the new class of young attorneys arrived, the top-ranked partners got the first pick of talent. The cruelest partners were stuck with the weakest squads.

I didn’t see any Mr. Hydes turn into Dr. Jekylls overnight. My aim was more modest: I wanted to give partners a reason to adjust their rituals. If they wanted talented squads, then they needed to be kinder to the people below them—or at least less callous. Some of them started to shape-shift, and instances of incivility dropped.

Reputations need to have repercussions. When you don’t have control over incentives, there are other ways to create consequences for bad actors. A while back I was advising a financial services company when a woman named Kathy was promoted to lead a new squad with a colleague named Colin. They hadn’t met before, and four different people warned her not to trust him—he was going to steal credit for her ideas and dump unwanted tasks on her. In their first one-on-one, Kathy shared the reputational feedback: “I don’t know whether it’s true or not. But if this is who you are, you are _not_ going to like working with me.” She was making it clear that his old rituals would be counterproductive with her. Over the year and a half that they collaborated, Colin went out of his way to share credit, volunteer for grunt work, and mentor junior people on the squad. He hadn’t liked his reflection and decided to make a concerted effort to change his image.

Ultimately, accountability starts at the top. You can judge a squad’s vibe by how people with power and status treat those who lack it. In too many squads, influential people fail to walk the talk. Merely hearing stories about leaders violating values can be enough to discourage newcomers from being good teammates.

In toxic cultures, stars get rewarded for results even if they wreck relationships. Abuse is seen as a price to pay for high performance. In healthy cultures, no level of individual excellence justifies undermining people. You’re not a high performer if you don’t elevate others. As organizational psychologist John Amaechi put it to me, “Culture is defined by the worst behavior tolerated.”

### Double Standards

The U.S. women’s soccer squad was aligned on their values and behavioral standards. They still needed a system for holding teammates accountable. I heard a lot of grumbling about hypocrisy—some stars expected their teammates to show up on time and wear team gear but didn’t always follow suit themselves. The squad had sometimes given their best players a pass, regardless of whether they upheld their principles. Certain stars had an implicit license to become prima donnas, deviate from core values, and dismiss young voices. As a co-captain, Megan Rapinoe decided to break that norm. She told me that as she reflected on the feedback from junior players, her attitude changed from “Whatever, we can just show up and play” to “The culture is not working for everyone.” She told her teammates she could be so task-focused that she came across as self-focused, and she was working on being less of a bulldozer so that she could hear their perspectives.

The most effective leaders are prototypical of the group and prosocial toward the group. Prototypicality is about exemplifying the squad’s core principles, and prosociality is about putting the collective purpose above personal interests. The net effect is often a team identity shift from _us vs. them_ to _all for one_.

When I asked the players to name their most prototypical and most prosocial teammates, one name consistently rose to the top. Becky Sauerbrunn had been a captain before, but she didn’t think she was qualified to be a leader—she wasn’t the biggest star or the loudest voice, and she’d never aspired to take the reins. I told her that the ideal leader is not the candidate who covets the role; it’s the person who’s earned the respect of the group.[[*7]](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-040 "footnote") A few months later, the coach elevated her back to captain.

Becky started holding veterans accountable by gently ribbing them when they failed to live up to the principles. _I see your popcorn in the break room…if it happens again, I’m calling your mom._ She singled out young players who were going above and beyond to leave it better and do their sh*t. _Love your devotion to the data, Moneyball._ The message came through quiet and clear: If you want to be a valued member of this squad, you need to respect our values.

There was a noticeable shift in their vibe. In summer 2020, the general manager reached out to share that the younger players were speaking up when veterans failed to uphold their values. “The fear of angering the loud minority,” the manager said, “was null.” And they had eradicated much of the nastiness—not just toward teammates but toward others as well. “You held up a mirror,” she noted, and “the staff is commenting that players are so much nicer to them than they have ever been. LITTLE WINS!”

They continued to change their rituals. The players made a commitment to give each other feedback about whether they were living up to the team’s principles. In 2021, Becky wrote me to say that they had an unusual number of athletes open up at training camp. They had spirited debates about how to evolve their behavioral standards and hold each other accountable—and the disagreements weren’t primarily between veterans and newcomers.

At the 2021 Olympics in Tokyo, the team ended up with a bronze medal. They knew they’d have to make some changes to get their performance—and culture—where it needed to be. Following the Olympics, some seasoned veterans retired and younger players stepped up. But before things could get better, they would get worse.

The team reached a new low at the 2023 World Cup, losing in the round of sixteen—their earliest exit ever. They continued making changes to put culture front and center. They brought in a new coach, Emma Hayes, who placed a strong emphasis on shared values and high standards. “To be a great head coach, you better be a great psychologist,” she said. The players doubled down on accountability, respect, and ownership, giving candid feedback and addressing tensions directly instead of avoiding them. In 2024, they came home from Paris with the Olympic gold medal.

How much of a difference did our work together make? It’s possible that it didn’t move the needle at all. Along with a coaching change, they had a fresh squad; of the 18 players who won in Paris, only 8 were on the team in Tokyo. But I hope it helped. Over the past five years, a number of players have reached out to tell me that they continue to reference the values and behaviors that we created and hold one another accountable for them. Improving their culture shifted their vibe, and their vibe lifted their game.

Building a great squad is not about minimizing the differences between people. It’s about channeling their differences toward a higher purpose.

A little profanity disinhibits us, helping us to marshal more of our mental and physical resources. It’s also a mark of authenticity—it signals a willingness to put candor above courtesy. And it reveals something about the vibe in a squad. In a study of over ten million emails at a tech company, people who cursed at significantly different rates than their colleagues were more likely to quit. The profanity mismatch was a sign that they weren’t fitting in.

[*4](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-043-backlink "footnote reference") What about the coach who gives the angry halftime speech? The best evidence suggests that it only works if the coach isn’t too mad—and isn’t usually mad. Screaming at the top of your lungs isn’t motivation; it’s a meltdown. Coaches who constantly berate people cross the line from demanding to demeaning. That doesn’t give squads a tailwind; it takes the wind out of their sails. Even for members who aren’t the target, merely witnessing disrespect is enough to disrupt focus and discourage teamwork.

[*5](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-042-backlink "footnote reference") Hundreds of studies show that when companies do mass layoffs, they end up being less profitable than similar firms that find other ways to lower costs—like executive pay cuts and unpaid employee leaves. Layoffs bleed talent and breed guilt and anxiety. They should be the last resort.

[*6](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-041-backlink "footnote reference") Some squads combined pal and companion cultures. That didn’t have consistent implications for squad speed or safety, but it did seem to discourage firefighters from taking unnecessary risks outside work—presumably because they knew their colleagues were counting on them.

[*7](019_c009_Chapter_9_All_for_On.xhtml#footnote-040-backlink "footnote reference") As Socrates observed in Plato’s _Republic_ , “The State in which the rulers are most reluctant to govern is always the best and most quietly governed, and the State in which they are most eager, the worst.” Or as Douglas Adams put it in _The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy_ series, “It is a well-known fact that those people who most _want_ to rule people are, ipso facto, those least suited to do it.” As a social scientist, I wouldn’t normally bet against the consensus of one of the great ancient Greek philosophers and one of the great modern British sci-fi writers. But technically it’s feeling a little reluctance—not extreme hesitations—that predicts empowering and effective leadership.
