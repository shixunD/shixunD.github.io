## Chapter 10

Everything Is Relative

Forming and Reforming Family Traditions

Tradition is a guide and not a jailer.

—W. Somerset Maugham

The month before I left for college, my parents announced that they were getting divorced. I was devastated to abandon my 11-year-old sister to a broken family. But I was also secretly relieved to escape halfway across the country. When Thanksgiving came around, my roommates traveled to be with their relatives. I decided to stay on campus alone. I was afraid my hometown would no longer feel like home.

Families are supposed to be our closest ties, yet they can also be the source of our deepest angst. We expect them to be there for us through our highest highs and our lowest lows. Yet not everyone is lucky enough to be born with relatives who make good pals and allies, let alone companions. Some families are estranged to the point that they no longer even associate. And many families fall apart, like mine did.

As soon as my parents separated, our family rituals disintegrated. We never took another family vacation. We never played another board game together. We never reunited for a holiday dinner with everyone. Every Thanksgiving, the emptiness gnawed at me. There was a gaping hole where my family was supposed to be. I wish I could tell my younger self that even though I couldn’t undo the divorce, there were things I could do to fill the void. Instead, I spent the next decade waiting impatiently. I hoped that one day when I had kids of my own, I would get the holiday—and the feeling—back. Eventually I did.

These days, Thanksgiving has special significance for me. It’s filled with family rituals. By the first day of November I’m already anticipating the sweet smell of Allison’s cornbread soufflé, the pure joy of playing with our kids, the friendly rivalry of family trivia, and the stubborn solidarity of holding out hope for the Detroit Lions. When I wake up on Thanksgiving morning, the first thing I do is check up on a family I’ve never met.

The matriarch of that family is a grandmother named Wanda Dench. Every Thanksgiving, she hosts her extended family in Arizona. They watch parades and football, eat turkey and green bean casserole, and do a modified gratitude circle, sharing what they’re thankful for and what they’re hopeful for. Those family occasions have been filled with bonding moments for Wanda and her grandchildren.

Jamal Hinton is the model grandson. During the pandemic, when Wanda lost someone close to her, he drove 45 minutes to drop off a gift basket and videos of people wishing her well. When she was diagnosed with cancer, he texted and called regularly to check in, and offered to run errands and bring her food. “I know I can count on him for anything,” Wanda says.

As I’ve watched them from afar, I’ve realized I had a narrow view of who makes up a family and how they interact. I didn’t need to wait until I had a family of my own to find what I was craving. I could have created new rituals with both sides of my existing family—or created a chosen family to form new rituals together. That’s how Jamal and Wanda fortified their bond.

Jamal and Wanda aren’t blood relatives. They met by accident in November 2016, when Jamal was in high school. He got a text inviting him to Thanksgiving dinner and didn’t recognize the number, so he asked who sent it. When the sender said she was his grandma, Jamal asked for proof. What came back was a picture of a complete stranger.

Wanda was trying to reach her grandsons. She didn’t realize one of their numbers had changed. Jamal was a proud class clown, so he couldn’t resist making a joke. He told her that she wasn’t his grandma and asked, “Can I still get a plate tho?” Instead of laughing it off or ignoring it, Wanda invited him over: “Of course you can. That’s what grandmas do…feed everyone.” Jamal posted the exchange on Twitter, where it went viral.

It could’ve easily ended there. Wanda already had six grandchildren, and Jamal already had a grandma. Wanda had her annual Thanksgiving tradition, and Jamal had his own. But they were both open to change. Jamal’s mom encouraged him to go even though that meant missing their own dinner. He took a leap of faith and accepted the invite to spend the holiday with a group of strangers.

When Jamal walked in the door, Wanda’s husband, Lonnie, welcomed him with a big hug. They laughed about the fact that Jamal forgot to bring the pumpkin pie he promised. They exchanged life stories, and Jamal sought Wanda’s advice on his future. “I just got a vibe from her that made me feel at home,” Jamal explained.

They decided to make their accidental Thanksgiving meal an annual tradition. For the past decade, every time they go around the table to share what they’re grateful for, Wanda says family—which includes Jamal. He shares that sentiment: “I consider her my grandma.”

Every November, seeing their latest updates warms my heart. After ten years of following their posts, I finally reached out to them. I couldn’t stop smiling as they told me about all the traditions they’ve created together. They don’t just see each other in November—they’ve done family game nights as well as parties for the Fourth of July, Halloween, and the Super Bowl. Each Thanksgiving, their dinner looks different. The dishes and activities vary, and so does the cast of relatives, friends, and neighbors in their makeshift family unit.

Sadly, in April 2020, Wanda’s husband, Lonnie, passed away. That Thanksgiving the family put an empty plate in front of Lonnie’s seat, lighting a candle in his honor. Then in 2024, Wanda was diagnosed with breast cancer. They had to FaceTime instead of gathering in person for Thanksgiving. The next year, Jamal’s aunt hosted Wanda’s family, and for 2026 he vowed to learn to cook so he could host both of their families and some friends. “Traditions keep love alive, but we keep getting curveballs,” Jamal reflected. “We’re ready for anything.”

Jamal is living the lesson I failed to learn in my twenties. We don’t always have the power to keep our families the same. But there are things we can do to keep our families close even when they change.

What makes families tight is not the consistency of their routines. It’s their commitment to forming and reforming meaningful rituals. Families vibe when they strike a balance between preserving old traditions and inventing new ones. A key to strong bonds is finding an equilibrium between continuity and change.

### Unconventional Conventions

Traditions are recurring rituals imbued with meaning. We might practice them frequently or at regular intervals, but they’re anything but routine. Routines are fixed; they often constrain our connections. _Toys must be cleaned up before bedtime_ destroys a fort and short-circuits fun with friends. _Dinner is always at six sharp_ is a recipe for teen rebellion. Traditions are flexible; they expand our sense of closeness. Whereas routines are organized around “this is what needs to be done,” psychologist Barbara Fiese explains, traditions communicate “this is who we are.”

Along with forging a family identity, traditions offer stability. They make memories that link us to the past and rituals that keep us connected in the future. Many families invent traditions around big annual events. For wedding anniversaries, some couples opt for companionship by redoing their speeches and re-creating the meal as a reward. For birthdays, I know one family that goes into pal mode by eating cake for breakfast and another that does an ice cream crawl instead of lunch… _pal a la mode._ Families get closer around more frequent traditions too. Some families become allies by holding weekly or monthly “family council” meetings, giving the kids a say in setting goals and planning activities. And for sporadic activities like airport pickups, most families default to the companion tradition of hugging, but some add a pal dimension. The best one I’ve seen is the video of two brothers, Owen and Ethan, who decided to prank their grandma by showing up in enormous inflatable tyrannosaurus rex costumes…but she got word of the plan and surprised them by walking up in a T. rex costume of her own.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that when families gather for holidays, the more traditions they have, the closer they feel. Holiday traditions can even have a bonding effect in families that don’t get along well. It doesn’t matter whether they’re opening presents, decorating trees, or going to church on Christmas, saying prayers at Shabbat dinner, worshipping ancestors at spring or fall festivals, or setting off fireworks, watching the ball drop, and sharing resolutions around New Year’s.

Strong traditions connect families on multiple wavelengths. On Thanksgiving, for example, families act like allies when they walk or run the turkey trot together, pals when they play pin-the-waddle-on-the-turkey, and companions when they read notes from an appreciation jar that’s been collecting messages all year. These kinds of rituals can prevent hostility and promote harmony by placing people in cooperative roles. When Grandpa takes his treasured turkey-carving post, he’s less likely to turn the knives on Uncle Leonard.

Traditions don’t just bring families together in the moment—they link us before and afterward too. In a study of Diwali rituals, it wasn’t just during the holiday festival itself that families vibed. As they cleaned and decorated together to prepare for the event, they felt more connected. The bond outlives the tradition: Nostalgia motivates families to reminisce, which brings them closer and motivates the next generation to continue traditions, augmenting their significance. Anticipating, participating in, and remembering traditions together signals and strengthens our sense of family as an important, unique unit.

As long as we agree on the symbolic significance of family traditions, they unite us. Yet if one person’s revered ritual is another’s tedious routine, that disconnect can drive us apart. As kids become teenagers, they aren’t always into matching pajamas, morning sunrises, or mandatory attendance at religious services. There’s evidence that college students feel less belonging in their families when they don’t find meaning in the holiday, vacation, or cultural traditions that their mothers hold dear.

Aligning on important traditions can protect families from adversity. It’s well-documented that the more meaning teens find in their family rituals, the more connected they feel—and the less depressed and anxious they are. When families struggle with alcoholism, if they maintain meaningful dinner, weekend, holiday, and vacation rituals, parents are less likely to pass alcoholism down to the next generation. When divorced parents maintain cooperative relationships and rituals, kids do better in school. And during the pandemic, families that sustained or adjusted shared mealtime, exercise, and bedtime rituals were more resilient, less lonely, and more cohesive. We don’t know if these are causal effects—families that already operated as allies, pals, and companions might have been more committed and creative around their traditions. But we do know that traditions confront chaos with structure and disrupt discord with solidarity. They communicate that even in the face of problems, we can still gain control and connection.

As families go through life cycles, some lose grandparents, aunts, or uncles, while others gain stepparents and stepsiblings or half-siblings. Kids gain independence, grow up, move out, and get married. New people enter our lives as in-laws and grandchildren arrive. New people might enter the family unit as our friends become like sisters or brothers to us—and aunts or uncles to our kids.

Close families are complex adaptive systems. To strengthen and sustain their bonds, they don’t just build traditions. They transform their traditions through times of transition. They often begin with small moments—and there’s nowhere better to start shifting the vibe than the dinner table.

### Beyond Food Fights

As our kids started elementary school, I noticed our dinner conversations veering toward achievement. _What grade did you get on the test? Did you score a goal in soccer?_

Most parents say that one of their top priorities is to raise kids who are caring and kind. Yet most kids believe that their parents value their success above all else. Kids don’t learn what matters to adults by listening to what we say. They discover our values by observing what captures our attention. If we want our kids to be kind, we need to show them it’s important to us.

I decided it was time to introduce a new family tradition. Every Friday at dinner, we started asking our kids, “Who did you help this week?”

At first, they struggled to come up with even trivial examples and cracked jokes instead. _I helped my teacher by not hiding another stuffed rat in her drawer._ But after a few weeks, they weren’t just remembering daily moments of kindness. They were recognizing and looking for opportunities to help—and volunteering to share them before we even asked. _Today I helped Frank the Tank find food! She’s our class turtle and I think she likes me._ Our dinner conversations were suddenly bursting with energy, and we felt closer than ever.

A few weeks later, Allison suggested expanding the tradition by asking our kids another question: “Who helped you?”

I didn’t get it. _I want to raise givers, not takers!_ She explained that she wanted our kids to choose their friends wisely. Instead of gravitating to classmates who were popular, they might start hanging out with peers who were caring. It made a mark. A decade later, Joanna ended up writing one of her college essays about how our Friday dinner tradition taught her to seek out friends who shared our family value of kindness.

Most families spend more time having meals together than any other activity. Although a typical family dinner is only about 18 minutes, many studies have demonstrated that the more often families eat together, the tighter their bonds and the higher their well-being. The sensation of food is amplified when it’s shared, and connection grows as we share it. When my son Henry eats something delicious, he turns to us and says with a big grin, “You have to try this! It’s _so_ good!” There’s no better bid for connection than conveying that you want someone else to experience your joy.[[*1]](020_c010_Chapter_10_Everythin.xhtml#footnote-052 "footnote") During meals, we also concoct our own family dialects—custom vocabularies of slang and inside jokes that no one else understands. I’ve come across families that refer to butter as _toast lotion_ , Parmesan cheese as _spaghetti sprinkles_ , and tongs as _snappy snap grabbers_. Communicating in a secret language binds us as a special group.

Eating as a family can also buffer against the emotional costs of conflict. Sharing a family meal doesn’t stop teenagers from fighting with their parents and siblings, but it does contain the fallout. Even if teens still resent having Instagram blocked for finishing homework late, dinnertime together helps reconnect them with family, restoring their sense of structure and solidarity. Teenage children become slightly less depressed after their families start having dinner together more frequently.

That said, regular meals aren’t a panacea. For parents with young kids, they’re a common source of chaos. As with other connections, quality is more important than quantity. Even one enjoyable family meal a week can be enough to prevent relationship problems. Eating together more often is less beneficial for families that aren’t close. It’s possible to reset the vibe, though, by turning meals into more meaningful traditions.

A ritual without meaning becomes a mind-numbing routine. Kids don’t want to recount the minutiae of their day. They don’t want to talk about what they’re grateful for every night either. _I’m thankful that my sister didn’t bite me today._ Psychologists have shown that gratitude exercises are more powerful when we only do them once a week. Spacing it out makes it less of a chore. It prevents the ritual from becoming a stale routine—and gives us time to find new reasons to be thankful. And my own research has shown that it’s more motivating to reflect on kindness than gratitude. Counting our blessings can leave us feeling like passive receivers; counting our contributions reminds us that we actively make a difference.

The Friday kindness questions were a powerful tradition when our kids were in elementary and middle school. Research suggests that an ideal time to reinforce values is between ages six to twelve—that’s a formative period when many kids are crystallizing a strong sense of self (and a few are starting to rebel). As our kids became teenagers, we evolved our rituals. To reinforce kindness, we started giving them more responsibilities for taking care of their younger siblings and our cats.[[*2]](020_c010_Chapter_10_Everythin.xhtml#footnote-051 "footnote") To encourage our kids to be kinder to themselves, we started doing occasional failure dinners, going around the table to share our recent mistakes and what they taught us. We also invited our kids to contribute their own topics to dinner discussions. _Where in the world do you most want to travel? Do you hear the words in your head when you read? Do you think books should ever be banned? What careers do you think each of your siblings will choose?_

Revamping our conversation rituals can revive family dinner traditions—and creative questions can spark joy and build bonds. The Family Dinner Project is a nonprofit that offers a whole library of novel conversation starters for meals. Instead of _How was school?_ they recommend _If you were the principal, what would you change?_ To build curiosity and empathy, they propose _What makes someone a good friend?_ and _If your pets could talk, what do you think they’d say?_ For fun, there’s _What’s your favorite silly face to make?_ To identify possible traditions, they suggest _What’s your favorite thing to do as a family?_

Some of the most evocative questions revolve around family history. Do you know how your parents met—and how they chose your name? What about where your grandparents grew up? And who in your family do you look and act most like?

In a series of studies, psychologists asked kids these kinds of questions. The more family stories they knew, the higher their well-being, both individually and collectively. The kids had higher self-esteem, lower anxiety, and fewer behavior problems. And their families were closer—they had stronger emotional bonds. Family stories create a secure base for kids, showing them that they belong to a group that existed in the past and will continue in the future.

But chatterboxes beware. Kids might not always be excited to listen as their elders drone on. My daughter Elena coined a term for it: _oldwashed_. It’s when getting older brainwashes you into the most boring conversationalist ever. We’ve added pal energy by turning family history into a trivia game. _Which family member got stuck behind a manure truck on a drive to Florida?_ And we’ve brought companionship by swapping embarrassing stories. _Did I ever tell you about the time I sprinted through an airport with yesterday’s underwear in my pant leg? How about when I dyed my hair maize and blue for the Michigan game, but it melted in the sun and turned green, and the crowd around me booed because they thought I was rooting for our rival Michigan State?_

Families are more cohesive and adaptable when they balance dreaming about tomorrow with reminiscing about yesterday. The most helpful family stories also aren’t solely uplifting or depressing; they feature both highlights and hardships. Balancing successes with setbacks conveys that life has ups and downs, and we can face them together. And one of the best ways to develop resilience is to renovate our traditions beyond the dinner table.

### Breaking with Tradition

How many of your family traditions today can be traced back to your childhood, or to your ancestors? In a survey, 81 percent of people reported continuing an old family tradition with their own kids. Nearly as many—74 percent—introduced new family traditions. Restoring or devising traditions together can bring joy and build strength.

Many families launch traditions that foster companionship to cope during tough times. After losing loved ones, some families keep their memories alive on their birthday by doing activities they loved or cooking their favorite meal. In one family, when his wife was halfway through chemotherapy, a man celebrated with half a cake, saving the whole cake for the end of treatment.

Different cultures offer inspiration for new traditions. When I travel abroad, the pal traditions jump out right away. In Brazil, I learned that some families host surprise parties the day before or after a birthday. In the Netherlands, families pick names out of a hat for winter holiday gift exchanges and write silly poems about each other to read out loud. In Australia, when the song “Nutbush City Limits” plays, families are expected to burst out into a synchronized line dance. In Venezuela on Christmas Eve, many families roller-skate to church.

I’m a fan of bringing creativity to weddings. Some of the most entertaining traditions blend pal and ally rituals. In Slovenia, the bride’s family might block the groom’s road to the wedding with wooden logs, demanding that he prove his worth by sawing through them. At some Indian weddings, the bride’s family is expected to steal the groom’s shoes as he walks up to the altar, while his relatives fend them off. If the bride’s family succeeds, they get to announce a ransom to return them.

Every time someone new joins our family, it’s an opportunity to ask about their favorite traditions. Fiancés, in-laws, stepparents, and stepsiblings are all potential sources of fresh rituals. We can also look to the past to identify traditions that have been lost in the present. After watching _Stranger Things_ , my kids said they wished they’d grown up in the 1980s. They felt nostalgic for an era they’d never experienced, when kids coordinated plans by walkie-talkie and rode their bikes to the arcade. When we asked our kids how they’d change one of our existing traditions to make our lives more like Hawkins, Indiana, they unanimously voted to ban phones from the table.

The evidence is clear that digital devices interfere with family communication and connection. Several dozen experiments have demonstrated that banning screens from meals increases conversation quality, enjoyment, and closeness. (It also reduces mindless overeating, making sure we don’t ignore the feeling that we’re full.) But let’s not throw out the baby with the bathwater. Screens only kill our vibe when they suck us into separate worlds and lead us to snub each other. Devices don’t have to breed detachment. Looking at the same screen can actually bring us closer. Several studies show that when families watch TV and movies together, they feel more connected—particularly if they don’t have the same social circles. Co-viewing creates shared experiences, signals shared interests, and can even lower our heart rates.

When it comes to screens, active engagement is better than passive consumption. One of our favorite family traditions is watching _Survivor_ together. We’ve activated pal and ally energy by competing against each other in one fantasy league and playing as a team in another, where the winner gets a gold crown to keep until the next season. Our kids also decided we should play our own family Survivor game—they designed the challenges, and I got voted out in a blindside orchestrated by Elena. We videoed key moments and the kids teamed up to make a highlight reel afterward. They had so much fun that they started making their own short films together, and watching them has become another family tradition. The best traditions breed new ones.

During the pandemic, our kids were cooped up in online school for a full year. To give them something to look forward to, we started a daily tradition of playing Mario Kart online with my sister and brother-in-law. We FaceTimed during each match so we could talk (read: trash talk). Research indicates that online games are a form of “digital glue” that can help to strengthen and sustain ties with family members who don’t live nearby. When families play video games together, they feel closer—especially if they usually have poor communication.[[*3]](020_c010_Chapter_10_Everythin.xhtml#footnote-050 "footnote")

My sister had twins later that year. As soon as they were old enough, we brought them into the Mario Kart tradition. I’ve loved watching my kids bond with their aunt, uncle, and cousins as they throw banana peels in their path.

### Special Relativity

The bonding options multiply when family gatherings go from on-screen to in-person. Connecting with extended family can expand our sense of belonging. But many family reunions are sadly short on banana peels. Too often, instead of having fun together, you have a mess. A baby cousin poops on the carpet. A nephew brings his brother’s ex as a date. Uncle Leonard slashes Grandpa’s tires.

Family reunions are very different for Melanie and Wayne Fischer’s grandkids. Their annual gathering in South Dakota is one of the highlights of their childhood. They call it Cousin Camp. Every summer, the Fischers invite all their grandkids five and older to their farm. As many as a dozen grandkids stay from Thursday to Sunday. There are two big rules: no parents, no screens.

At Cousin Camp, bedrooms are converted into cabins. Each morning, the cousins wait for the bell to ring, then leap out of bed for the flag-raising ceremony and stretches. The days are packed with arts and crafts, music, swimming, sports, and a talent show. They go on field trips, do kitchen duty, and hold campfires at night. It formally ends with a Sunday picnic, ballgame, and performance—the cousins sing songs, do cheers, and present their personal highlight reels. Throughout the year, the cousins love reminiscing about last summer and counting the days until the next one.

When I came across Cousin Camp online, I thought it was one of a kind. It turns out that a number of people do their own versions of it, either with relatives or chosen families. It’s a natural venue for face-to-face conversations over meals as well as side-by-side activities like board games, which are ideal for ally and pal bonding across generations.[[*4]](020_c010_Chapter_10_Everythin.xhtml#footnote-049 "footnote")

Part of the power of the tradition is that it’s not a constant. Cousin Camp has to evolve over time. To keep the pal activities fresh, there are grandparents who vary the theme from year to year, from a nature camp centered on hiking, fishing, and rocks and gems to a creative camp focused on making tie-dye shirts, designing puzzles, and building model airplanes. To keep teenagers engaged, some families run separate camps for older and younger cousins—or transform the tradition from camp to an annual trip to the mountains. Other families have older cousins graduate from campers to counselors, where they become allies, pals, and companions to their younger cousins.

Cousin Camp gives kids a chance to bond with their cousins and grandparents, away from their parents. We all know the importance of grandparents in instilling values and providing stability. Yet we overlook the unique roles that cousins can play. This is another ingredient in Cousin Camp’s success: a wide-open field of wavelengths.

Other than our sisters and brothers, cousins are the only blood relatives we get at similar ages and life stages, and there’s no sibling rivalry—we don’t have to fight with them for parental attention or resources. That makes them natural pals. They offer familiarity without friction and bonds without baggage.

Not everyone wins the cousin lottery. But at different phases of our lives, it’s common to vibe with cousins on different wavelengths. As we move into adulthood, they evolve from playmates to allies. The family tie offers a basic level of built-in trust.[[*5]](020_c010_Chapter_10_Everythin.xhtml#footnote-048 "footnote") When Allison and I were ready to buy a house, we knew our mortgage broker wouldn’t screw us over, because she’s my cousin. _No one would dare risk the wrath of Grandma._ When we face crises, we frequently turn to cousins as companions. It’s common for cousins to provide emotional support and become closer after losing a parent, grandparent, aunt, or uncle. And as they start families of their own, cousins frequently become the “kinkeepers” who hold extended families together through planning holiday meals, hosting reunions, and sending pebbles on group texts.

Relatively speaking, cousin connections are usually low-maintenance relationships. They tend to occupy a sweet spot of togetherness—neither disengaged nor enmeshed. As one pair of social scientists put it, cousins are “close but not too close.” The obligation to stay in regular touch is minimal, and reconnecting can be seamless. My cousin Mike taught me the rules of football when I was six (“I don’t know what a line of scrimmage is, but you need one”) and introduced me to Allison when I was 23. In between, I only remember seeing him outside family gatherings once, when he took me to a Lions game. It doesn’t matter how long the tie has been dormant; when they reconnect over a meal, at a reunion, or on a trip, cousins can often skip past associates and interact as allies, pals, or companions.

### Dynamic Duos

I used to think family activities should happen together as one unit. Now I understand that not all family traditions need to involve the whole family. Close families derive strength from the sum of their parts—they’re built on strong pair bonds. For connection, there’s nothing like one-on-one time. I stumbled into this realization at the end of the pandemic.

To make up for lost travel, I started inviting my kids to join me on work trips. Henry picked L.A., where we went to an amusement park with a second cousin once removed—and another amusement park with one of my closest childhood friends and his kids. Henry bonded with them as they faced their fear of roller coasters together.

Each trip has brought us closer to blood relatives and chosen family—and each other. It’s also given us new memories to savor and laugh about…which mostly involve my kids laughing at me. On one trip Joanna decided we should go whitewater rafting in class 4 rapids. I was the only one to fall out, and a photographer managed to capture it. Whenever Joanna sees extended family, she asks if they’ve seen my worst backflip.

![Photo of a group of six people whitewater rafting in a rubber raft on churning class 4 rapids, with one person in the center flipping backward out of the boat, legs in the air, while the others in helmets and life jackets sit bracing with paddles amid the crashing waves.](assets/75_WhiteRiverRafting_r1.jpg)

Taking each of my kids on a trip has become an annual tradition. When their siblings aren’t there, kids tend to be on their best behavior—they have a way of rising or sinking to the maturity of the people around them. And the one-on-one time gives our rituals a chance to evolve and improve. If a trip is too time-consuming or expensive, we can get the same benefit without going anywhere. Even small one-on-one traditions at home can strengthen bonds—which strengthen the whole family.

Neuroscientists find that when a parent and a child focus their undivided attention on a book together, their brains synchronize. The same neural coupling happens when a parent and an infant play together, and it’s usually the infant’s interests that guide the adult’s focus. This is true for parents of any gender and families of any size. Historically, mothers carried most of the load, but today dads are spending about triple the time with their kids that their dads did with them. When father figures are engaged, kids show better cognitive development, boys have fewer behavioral problems, and young women have fewer emotional problems.

When parents have multiple kids, equity is essential. Nearly half of adults believe their parents had a favorite child. One journalist deadpans that the rest of the parents “may simply be doing a better job of concealing things.”[[*6]](020_c010_Chapter_10_Everythin.xhtml#footnote-047 "footnote") Parental favoritism is a major culprit behind sibling rivalry, and spending separate one-on-one time with each kid is a tradition that helps parents treat siblings fairly.

Pair bonding is important for siblings too. For toddlers, pretend play isn’t just pal fun; using their imaginations together helps them understand each other’s thoughts and feelings. When siblings spend quality time together without their parents, they can come up with their own traditions—from invented games to secret languages and inside jokes. There’s often a turning point toward becoming closer allies and companions when siblings are in their early twenties. With maturity and space, siblings are able to rebuild their rituals and reset their relationships. Some sisters and brothers become stronger allies by working together professionally or closer companions by raising their own families together. Others sustain their pal bonds by doing an annual Sibling Olympics or an April Fool’s prank contest. _I recommendHide Justin._ _In that game, siblings hid a postcard of Justin Timberlake in hilarious places—they found it in cereal boxes and even frozen into ice._

Of all the pair bonds in a family, one has an outsized influence on the overall family bond. It’s the connection between parents. It forms the foundation of the other family connections, and in Western cultures, it’s evolved dramatically over time. In the early days of America, marriages were pragmatic partnerships. Most people didn’t expect to be companions with their spouses; they saw them first and foremost as allies in raising a family. As we’ve evolved our ideals, we’ve opened the door to more fulfilling bonds. Yet we’ve also placed a heavy burden on our closest connections. As therapist Esther Perel puts it, “We turn to one person to provide what an entire village once did.” There’s evidence that when people describe their significant other as their best friend, they feel more companionship but receive less support than if they have a separate best friend. Ultimately, too many marriages fail (or fail to happen in the first place) because people expect to rely on one partner for everything.

You can vibe with your partner on every wavelength, but they shouldn’t be your _only_ associate, pal, ally, or companion. And it’s not possible to be in sync all the time. It puts a lot of strain on a marriage to demand a partner who’s perfect at fixing the printer and cleaning the cat litter and knowing that you don’t really want socks for Valentine’s Day and disciplining the kids and picking movies and hosting guests and listening to all your complaints and quickly admitting that socks were a terrible, horrible, no good, very bad gift.

Families cope with these challenges more effectively if parents work on their own bond together before kids enter the picture. In a course called Family Foundations, expecting parents learn to resolve conflicts together as allies, support each other as companions, and develop morning, mealtime, and bedtime rituals for daily connection. Remarkably, when couples are randomly assigned to take the course before their first child arrives, their families are closer a decade later. There’s more connection and less conflict between parents as well as siblings. The course proves especially helpful for expecting parents with rocky relationships. If you already have kids, have no fear. Traditions are not tasks to check off on a to-do list; we can introduce them into our interactions anytime. It’s never too late to start hiding Justin.

As life expectancies trend upward and birth rates trend downward, our family trees look increasingly like beanstalks. They may be tall, but they’re narrow. Shrinking immediate families leave us with much smaller extended families. If our parents have two kids instead of three, and so do we, our kids will miss out on one brother or sister—but they’ll have five fewer first cousins.

However many relatives we have, there’s no guarantee that we’ll vibe with them. Some relatives make great pals but poor companions. Some relationships will always be ambivalent, and some conflicts will never be resolved. Instead of having a closed family circle, we’re better off staying open. Welcoming new members into a chosen family allows us to build our own village. That’s what Jamal Hinton and Wanda Dench have realized.

In 2022, Wanda was missing her husband and her mother and father. She decided to keep them close by getting a tattoo of three shooting stars on her arm to remind her of the courage, integrity, and love they taught her. Jamal has a tattoo of a lion, and when she called him at the last minute before her appointment, he dropped everything to accompany her.

The following year, Wanda moved to the Arizona mountains, two hours from Jamal. He made the drive to join her family for Thanksgiving, and not long after that he moved to Florida for a job. But they both found themselves craving more time with the families they’d built by blood and by choice. Jamal moved back to the Phoenix area…and Wanda decided to do the same. She beams as she tells me their homes are only five minutes apart. Jamal says the drive only takes him four minutes. They’re looking forward to regular game nights and dinners, and—if Jamal has his way—matching Thanksgiving tattoos.

If we live far away from the friends and relatives we see as family, the majority of our gatherings may already be behind us. Establishing and evolving traditions is how we stay connected in our scarce time together.
