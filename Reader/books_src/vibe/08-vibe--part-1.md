# Part 1

Sparking Connection

The Three Myths of Chemistry and Closeness

A few years ago, I ran into an acquaintance at a conference. He’d been generous in recommending my work, but I didn’t know him well—we’d only chatted a handful of times. When we sat down to catch up, this happened:

Acquaintance: Come stay with us next time you’re in L.A.

Me: I wouldn’t want to impose.

Acquaintance: Impose? We love hosting our friends!

Me: Wait…we’re friends?!

Acquaintance: I thought we were good friends!

In romantic relationships, we have rituals to define the relationship. _What are we? What’s up with us?_ That conversation is frequently a formality—we’re labeling what we’re already feeling. In platonic relationships, though, we lack the vocabulary to discuss where we stand. And we often don’t see eye to eye.

When sociologists ask us to list our friends, only about half name us back. _Ouch. Half of our friends don’t see us that way._ It sounds bleak. But maybe the issue isn’t that our friendships are asymmetric—it’s that we have different ideas about what it means to be friends.

“Friend” is far too broad of a label. It says nothing about the kind of relationship you have. Some people solve that by distinguishing acquaintances from buddies from besties. Yet this overlooks the fact that you can be close in multiple ways.

If you’re like most people, you categorize relationships by life domains. You sort people into friends, relatives, neighbors, or colleagues. These terms describe how you’re connected, but reveal little about why. They fail to capture how some colleagues become friends—and what makes some friends feel like family.

My research for this book led to a revelation. It’s a way of understanding interactions that expands our potential for connection across all kinds of relationships. In reviewing evidence across a wide range of cultures, I’ve discovered that we can connect with people on four different wavelengths. We can vibe as associates, allies, pals, and companions.

Associates are transactional ties. Your primary reason for communicating is to exchange resources. Your interactions are based on reciprocity. Some of my associates are our neighbors—we take turns shuttling our kids to activities. My associates also include the programmers who manage my website and my distant cousin the dermatologist who treated my poison ivy rash. We’re joined by compatible needs.

Allies are partnerships based on purpose. You’re on a mission together. You might volunteer together, worship together, or launch a startup together. Among my allies are the members of my podcast team, the students in my research lab, the early readers who give feedback on my books, and the fellow travelers who write about behavioral science. We’re united around compatible goals.

Pals are alliances of amusement. You hit it off because you enjoy the same forms of fun and entertainment. Basically, you like to do the same stuff. If you’ve never hiked together, shopped together, seen a movie together, partied together, or decorated someone’s car with shaving cream together, you’re probably not pals. My pals are the people who share my love of games (anagrams, Mario Kart), comedy (_Seinfeld_ , _Billy Madison_), and sports (skiing, Ultimate Frisbee, padel…really anything but golf). We’re linked by compatible interests.

Companions are connections based on care. You confide in one another and rely on one another for emotional support. You go to them when sh*t hits the fan, and they lean on you when they’ve lost their fans. They don’t think it’s weird if you text them a family photo or FaceTime them randomly. My closest companions include my wife and kids, a handful of mentors, and my (remaining) friends from middle school. We’re attached by deep affection and compatible values.

Different wavelengths involve different priorities. With associates and allies, your primary focus is on the outcome of your interaction. Your connections feel worthwhile when you solve a problem or achieve a goal together. With pals and companions, you’re living in the moment—you’re there for the process itself. When your time together is delightful or meaningful, the vibe is rewarding regardless of the result.

We connect on different wavelengths for different reasons. We usually become associates and pals based on convenience. We find ourselves in situations where we can trade favors or discover shared hobbies. We gain allies and companions based on commitments. We choose to team up with people who are on a common path and confide in people with common principles. This helps to explain why many people have such heated conflict with a parent or sibling. You’re expected to be companions, but since you don’t choose blood relatives, you don’t always share that chemistry.

Of course, we have plenty of connections that cover multiple wavelengths. We’ll get to that in chapter 1.

Knowing the purpose behind our bonds is a mirror and a map. It helps us make choices and avoid conflict by clarifying where different people fit in our lives. You can text allies and call pals to share good news, but companions are more likely to hold space for bad news. Allies and associates don’t expect a wedding invitation, but companions will be hurt if they’re not in the wedding party…and pals should help plan your bachelor or bachelorette party.

Considering different wavelengths gives us new ways to vibe and equips us to manage the connections we already have. There isn’t time for everyone you meet to become more than an associate. Still, you can have great chemistry with associates if you’re on the same page about the fact that your exchanges are pragmatic. There’s no master plan to turn them into allies or companions. You just hope they’ll respond rapidly to a late-night text when you need a referral to a good plumber.

Similarly, we don’t need all our coworkers to be pals or companions. Many people are uncomfortable when their boss tries to friend them. In the language of _Severance_ , their “innies” like to keep things professional so their “outies” can let loose. Also, it can be nice to have companions and pals who aren’t allies. When there’s no task or objective looming over our heads, the only agenda item is the relationship itself. It’s all about enjoying the ride together.

Some anthropologists have argued that we can only handle about five people in our inner circles and ten to fifteen more close friends. Recognizing that our bonds exist on different wavelengths relaxes that constraint. Since caring connections are hard to make and maintain, most people don’t have a long list of companions. But there’s no natural limit on the number of allies we can team up with or how many pals we can enjoy.

Each wavelength adds something unique to our lives—one isn’t superior to another. Take it from studies spanning over 50,000 participants around the world: We’re happier when we have a balanced portfolio of interactions. Just as we all have multiple love languages, different bonds help us feel valued and add value in distinct ways. Loneliness is the absence of companions, joylessness is the absence of pals, and aimlessness is the absence of allies.

Have you ever noticed that people are often happier with their friends than their partners or kids? Psychologists find that it’s not because we prefer the company of our friends. It’s because we do different activities with them. We discipline our kids and do chores with our partners but plan fun with our friends. If we spent more family time in pal mode, we’d enjoy the vibe more.

Associates, allies, pals, and companions are types of connections, but they’re also modes of interaction. We’re doing an ally ritual when we enter a tennis tournament with our doubles partner and an associate ritual when we reimburse them for the entry fee. We’re doing a pal ritual when we meet them for drinks and a companion ritual when we express condolences for their loss. Interacting _like_ pals or companions doesn’t mean we _are_ pals or companions, but it can move us toward that kind of relationship.

We vibe when we connect on a common wavelength. Alignment creates chemistry, elevating the energy in our interactions. That paves the way to closeness, strengthening our bonds. We get along and grow tight when we see our relationship the same way—and communicate that way.

It’s possible to form strong bonds on any wavelength. We can be close with every kind of connection. The jump from acquaintances to companions may be steeper than the step to associates, allies, or pals. But you might have close associates, allies, and pals you regularly turn to—and more distant companions you rarely rely on.

Being on different wavelengths creates static and interference. We struggle to click when we don’t agree on the kind of connection we have or the kind we want to build. It’s not fun to be on the receiving end of unwanted advances from people trying to impose their ideal vibe on you. A high school pal suggests going into business together. _Liking you does not mean I would like to work with you._ A work ally tries to rope you into companionship by dumping his marital problems on you. _I am not your emotional support blanket._ Your car salesperson—a distant associate—starts texting to see when you’re free to hang out. _Code red! A palvader has entered the premises._[[*]](008_p001_Part_1_Sparking_Conn.xhtml#footnote-002 "footnote")

Research reveals that in both personal and professional interactions, being on different wavelengths is a recipe for feeling disappointed or even betrayed. In about half of our conversations, there’s a mismatch between what we want and what we get from others. Personally, I’ve sometimes mistaken pals for companions, only to feel let down when they don’t show up (or even show much concern) in a time of need. Part of growing up is learning that the pals we party with aren’t always suited to be the companions whose shoulders we cry on.

This is a starting point for sparking connection: finding someone who’s open to the same frequency. Usually without having to explicitly ask about it…unless you’re me and my L.A. acquaintance. In my mind we were distant allies, but he saw us as something else. That’s why I was so surprised when he invited me to stay with him. At lunch several months after our awkward chat, his wife asked if I thought _she_ was my friend. It became a running inside joke: _Are you my friend?_ That summer I ended up staying with them, and my new pal and I were up past midnight watching magic videos together, trying to crack the secret to a trick we’d loved.

Potential connections are all around us. We miss out on them when we put people in narrow boxes. _Not friend material_ keeps people at a distance. _We’re just fundamentally different_ can spur us to drop them from our lives. Of course, some people will never be more than acquaintances to us, and others might end up being our adversaries. But when we approach the people we meet as possible associates, allies, pals, or companions, we have more opportunities to vibe. Each wavelength offers different options for how to start off on the right foot.

What stands between us and better connections is our beliefs about what it takes to build them. We think we need to search for the right person, hit it off right away, and invest the right amount of time to bond, but these are all myths. It’s time to rethink our assumptions about who we’re compatible with, how fast chemistry happens, and how long closeness takes.
