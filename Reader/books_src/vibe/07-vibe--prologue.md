## Prologue

I love people, but for a long time, I struggled to connect with them.

In eighth grade, I met up with my friend group to play pickup basketball at a local gym. I’d saved up for a new ball—the official NBA edition. I was excited to bring it to the game, but after we played, I couldn’t find it.

Weeks later, I learned that one of my “friends” stole my ball, and the rest of them knew but didn’t bother to tell me. I was devastated.

The thief was my oldest friend—we were born in the same room at the same hospital two days apart. I was the one who’d introduced him to the rest of the group. We’d been close since kindergarten. Or so I’d thought.

On my first day of high school, I had a grand total of two friends left. One of the main things we had in common was not really having other friends. I set out to find my people, but I didn’t know where to look, and it certainly didn’t feel like they were looking for me. A typical conversation went like this:

Me: _Grapes of Wrath_ was the worst book ever.

Guy in literature class: Glad I didn’t do the reading then.

Me: Did you see _Friends_ last night?

Guy: Yeah, it was funny.

[end of conversation]

After a few months I stopped searching and settled into my niche as a diver on the high school swim team. I rarely hung out with my new teammates, but at least I’d found a place.

It had to get better in college—that’s where you’re supposed to make lifelong friends. I left Michigan and went halfway across the country for a fresh start. I felt optimistic: The pool of people was much bigger, and everyone was eager to connect. I followed all the clichés. _Get involved. Meet new people. Put yourself out there._ It seemed to be working. I racked up a solid list of acquaintances. Yet when it came time to find roommates for the following year, most people were noncommittal. I’d made surprisingly few real friends.

I kept trying. I’d been performing as a magician, so I started doing shows for student groups, but their enthusiasm vanished as soon as the sleight of hand was over. I tried going to parties, but I was too self-conscious to dance, the music blared too loud to say anything of substance, and the only people I could hear were close talkers who cornered me and drained my energy. I preferred hanging out in my dorm with my buddies Joey and Chandler. _Sadly, they weren’t in the market for another roommate. Also, they live in a TV._

After freshman year, I roomed with two guys from Michigan I’d gotten to know before college. Eventually I convinced them that instead of going to drunken parties, we should host board game nights. These gatherings were a magnet for fellow geeks in our dorm, who flocked to trivia matches and anagram battles. Although we had fun, we failed to form links that lasted beyond the semester. I kept asking myself: _Is this really it?_

I thought bonding was hard because I’m a shy introvert. I’m not antisocial—I’m just pro-quiet.

Then I learned I was doing it wrong.

My junior spring, I wandered into a course on personality psychology. After the first lecture, I got so absorbed in a conversation with a few of the other students that I was late to my next class. It wasn’t long before we were meeting up for dinners that went past midnight and waxing poetic about the meaning of life. It wasn’t just one group; I sat near different people every week and hit it off with almost everyone in earshot. Things were finally clicking.

The catalyst was our delightfully quirky Canadian professor, Brian Little. He was so introverted that after his lectures he darted out of the classroom to recharge by hiding in a bathroom stall. Yet the way he engaged with us was contagious. Taking his course was like joining a secret society. It changed how we talked to each other—I ended up having more deep discussions with those classmates than the rest of college combined. I’m still friends with a bunch of them today.

I couldn’t stop wondering why these interactions were so different. I finished the class with a mission: reverse-engineer the dynamic and figure out how to foster that kind of kinship. I had to know what it takes for people to vibe.

We often attribute vibes to specific people and places. _I love her vibe. This room has good vibes._ But a vibe isn’t just automatically there. It comes alive in our interactions.

A vibe is the quality of connection between people. The Latin root is _vibratio_ , for vibration. When we vibe, we’re in rhythm. It’s more than a feeling: Neuroscientists can trace our brainwaves and heartbeats synchronizing. Conversations pulse with energy. Thoughts soar. Trust grows. Time flies. The product is more than mutual attraction—it’s mutual transformation.

When we don’t manage to vibe, we make mistakes. Studies show that professional golfers miss more putts when they’re playing alongside peers who don’t share their politics. Pilots make more potentially catastrophic errors when they’ve never flown together. And surgeons have higher patient mortality rates when they’re operating with new teams. When the stakes are high, a vibe can become a matter of life and death. It even determines whether countries go to war or find peace.

A great vibe fuels happiness and success. It underpins our most memorable moments with our families and our most enjoyable evenings with our friends. It buffers against stress; there’s even evidence that if we’re standing with a friend, a hill looks less steep.

Vibing with other people propels our best work and our biggest breakthroughs, enabling us to do together what we can’t do alone. Chemistry between classmates spawned the sublime sound of Simon & Garfunkel, the comic genius of Tina Fey and Amy Poehler, and the technical elegance of the Apple computer. Synchrony between siblings gave us the lifesaving Mayo Clinic, the mind-bending _Matrix_ films, and the first skyscraping Kitty Hawk flight. The fusion of the Founding Fathers gave America the Declaration of Independence. Collaborations between couples spurred the development of treatments for diabetes and the discovery of radioactivity. _For Marie and Pierre Curie, the chemistry was literal as well as figurative._

Sometimes we vibe instantly and effortlessly. We sit next to someone on a bus and within minutes it feels like we’ve known them for years. We move into an apartment and feel immediately at home with our new neighbors. We leave a dinner party and find ourselves missing people who were strangers earlier that day.

But other times, no matter how hard we try, we can’t even build rapport. This is one of the great mysteries of social life: If we can vibe spontaneously with some of the people some of the time, why can’t we do it deliberately with more of the people more of the time?

Human connection is one of the most pressing problems in our world. In the U.S., loneliness has been rising steadily since the late 1970s. Today, the average American feels more isolated than the typical person anywhere in Europe. The proportion of U.S. adults who have no close friends quadrupled from 1990 to 2020. Research suggests that most of us would rather interact with a stranger who shares our political ideology than a friend who doesn’t. Although shared meals are a robust predictor of happiness, more and more of us are dining alone. Growing numbers of adults are relying on chatbots for conversation, connection, and emotional support. And teens are dating less and spending half as much time with friends as they did a decade ago—and millions now prefer their sycophantic AI companions over humans.

We’re lonely because we’re missing connection. We’re missing connection because we’re struggling with its foundation: interaction. The problem isn’t just that we’re having fewer interactions—we’re stumbling our way through them too. Replacing face-to-face conversations with digital screens can stunt our capacity to read social and emotional cues. Outsourcing communication to chatbots can leave us asleep at the wheel, lacking the aptitude to form genuine ties. Social skills are like muscles: without regular training, they atrophy. It’s been documented with astronauts coming back from isolation in space, prisoners coming out of solitary confinement, and the rest of us coming out of the pandemic. As places of worship wane and community clubs disappear, there are fewer chances to exercise those muscles.

But withering social skills are only part of the story. Social norms can also constrain our connections. In every culture, there are routines and scripts that govern our interactions. Ask strangers what they do, not what they love to do. Chat with colleagues about the weather, not their families. Talk with kids about their homework, not their crushes.

Sociologists call these patterns interaction rituals, and they can make or break a vibe. Successful interaction rituals fire us up and cement our connections. Finishing each other’s sentences on a first date. Riffing on each other’s ideas in a brainstorming session. Being in harmony with the whole crowd at a concert. Missing a friend so much that you sprint toward each other for a hug at the airport.

When interaction rituals are failed or forced, it’s an instant vibe killer. A parent complains that you never call…but never picks up the phone to call you. Your boss stands up in your weekly meeting and reads the presentation slides verbatim. A neighbor walks by and barely grunts hello.

Sadly, many of our modern rituals maximize convenience and comfort, yet deprive us of the chance to vibe. If we’re not careful, our lives can become endless loops of mindless exchanges. As we repeat shallow conversational routines, we become prisoners trapped in bad interaction ritual chains. You’ve probably seen it at family holiday gatherings. The norm is to steer the dialogue to food, traffic, or sports, avoiding not just current events but also our formative moments from the past and our foremost dreams for the future. We swap a slice of our humanity for a piece of predictability. Gobble gobble.

That’s a mistake I made repeatedly growing up. In eighth grade, my interactions with my friend group were confined to superficial communication at pickup games. When our basketball routine ended, so did our relationship. What I thought was a bond was actually a charade. _I still want my ball back, Alvin._[[*1]](007_pro_Prologue.xhtml#footnote-001 "footnote")

It turns out that even small rituals can reveal a great deal about connections. There’s evidence that you can predict a basketball team’s success just by counting how often the players touch after big plays. The more NBA players high-five, fist-bump, and bear-hug in an October or November game, the better their teams perform all the way through April. That subtle habit is a sign that they’re vibing. Looking back, it was missing from my basketball games. When I scored, my team didn’t cheer.

What happens when we stop going through the motions, and start exploring new ways to connect? That’s what Brian Little helped us do when we walked into his personality psychology classroom. I’ll never forget my first visit to office hours.

Brian: Hey Adam, how ya doing?

Me: Good, how are you?

Brian: No, _really_ : How are you doing?

[beginning of connection]

It was immediately clear that he cared—he genuinely wanted to know about my life. I confided that I was grappling with career indecision; I had no idea what I wanted to be when I grew up. In a matter of minutes, I was telling Brian about my upbringing in Michigan and my path from basketball benchwarmer to springboard diver. He joked about my passion for “controlled falling” and asked what my current hobbies might mean for my future plans.

A few days later, Brian sent me some articles that built on our discussion. Over the next few weeks, he kept delivering tiny treasures into my inbox. A study hot off the presses, a new class to consider, even a clip of an impressive Olympic dive he’d caught.

Brian was extending our vibe with an interaction ritual that came naturally to him. It’s called pebbling. The name comes from a ritual among penguins. When they’re courting potential mates, some penguins collect pebbles to present. They share smooth, shiny stones that are appealing and suitable for building a nest together.

The digital equivalent of pebbling is sharing memes, links, and reels with others. These aren’t trivial gestures—every pebble is a bid for connection. Pebbling is an act of care. It’s a modern version of the lost art of writing letters and sending postcards. It signals _I’m thinking of you._

Research shows that pebbling helps us solidify and sustain bonds—especially with people we don’t know well. What makes it meaningful is that it’s personal. You’re not spamming people with random junk from the internet; you’re taking the time to share something that reminded you of them. And you probably don’t realize how much it matters.

Think of someone you’ve met recently. You’re going to write them a note to make them feel appreciated. What would you say?

If you’re like most people, your instinct is to show warmth by writing a note filled with praise and enthusiasm. _You have cool energy. I enjoyed meeting you!_ But psychologists find that something else makes people feel more valued: mentioning a few tidbits you recall about them. _You have cool tattoos. I enjoyed learning about your passion for axolotls!_ A little memory flex shows that we listened—and we cared enough to remember. It makes others feel closer to us. Being friendly says _I like you_ ; being personal says _I see you_. It’s nice to send someone a pebble you love, it’s moving to find one they’ll love—and it’s bonding to share one you both love.

This is why pebbling is so powerful. To send someone an article or video that resonates, you have to remember their unique interests and experiences. That’s what Brian Little did. I couldn’t believe he’d kept track of so many specifics from our chat, let alone took the time to shoot his favorite finds my way. Throughout the semester, he asked novel questions and listened intently to the answers. _How did you get over your fear of public speaking to become a magician?_ He was like a detective searching for clues, except he was solving for connection instead of crime. He saw each of our lives as an exciting mystery to unfold together.

Brian shared my curiosity as we revisited pivotal scenes from my childhood and my joy as I uncovered new aspirations for my adulthood. He recommended books to read and people to meet based on one-off comments I’d forgotten making. He also gently steered me away from classes that wouldn’t be for me. _I don’t want to speak ill of a colleague, but that professor is the fourth most boring person on earth._ And in lectures, he dangled threads for all of us to pull. _Did you realize you don’t only get goosebumps when you’re cold? Many people have them in response to beautiful art, music, or poetry—they’re called aesthetic chills._

By modeling a more authentic, more effervescent form of communication, Brian inspired us to reinvent our rituals. We posed questions with more gravity. _What’s your biggest regret?_ We opened up with more levity. _Well, right now I regret making you uncomfortable by asking such a personal question._

The vibe in Brian’s class was so electric that my classmates and I carried the charge beyond the lecture hall. Several of my roommates were skeptical of psychology. Instead of just following the old script of asking about their day, I started sharing factoids with them. _Did you know that if you sprinkle lemon juice on their tongues in the morning,introverts salivate more than extraverts?_ Now they were fascinated, and it led to real dialogue about our personalities and eccentricities. It gave me aesthetic chills.

After taking Brian’s class, my career indecision vanished. I would become an organizational psychologist and study how to spark, strengthen, and sustain bonds.

That’s what I’ve been doing for the past two decades. I’ve been called by Olympic coaches to cultivate team chemistry and NASA leaders to help nurture trust on the space station. Although I still stumble in plenty of interactions, I’ve honed my ability to vibe with guests as a podcast host, audiences as a speaker, and students as a teacher. At Wharton, I teach a course on synchrony, and I’ve turned my classroom into a living lab for building community. I’m finally ready to share what I’ve learned about the art and science of successful connections. My younger self would be thrilled to know that like shooting and dribbling a basketball, vibing is a skill set that can be learned and taught. No matter how good you are at connecting, you can always get better—and help others improve too.

There are many books about specific types of ties, from dating and marriage to parenting and friendship to leadership and teamwork. My goal is to illuminate the ingredients for connection across all kinds of relationships. As a social scientist, as always, I started with the data—which challenged some of my basic assumptions about bonding. I created a framework to organize the core insights and then found stories to animate them.[[*2]](007_pro_Prologue.xhtml#footnote-000 "footnote")

The first section of _Vibe_ focuses on the catalysts for connection. I’ll introduce you to a crew of astronauts who learned to trust people they don’t like and a pair of sworn enemies who became best friends. They bust the myths that similarity is necessary for solidarity, chemistry happens quickly, and closeness takes time.

The second section examines steps for sustaining connections. You’ll meet a troupe of comedians who swear by months apart as a key to staying together, two classmates who preserved a pebble to maintain their friendship for nearly a century, a basketball star who cracked the code on helping teammates avoid meltdowns, and a therapist who saved a rock band by coaxing them to fight more. You’ll see how conflict can be harnessed, complicated ties can be handled, and sporadic touchpoints can have lasting benefits.

The third section explores strategies for scaling connection and finding belonging in groups. You’ll learn how to design great gatherings from a friend of mine whose parties led to dozens of marriages, how to build strong communities from an educator who turned a school into Hogwarts, and how to get squads in sync from my work with an elite team that didn’t get along. And a family that came together by accident will reveal a recipe for lifelong bonds.

* * *

![](assets/page_3_2.jpg)

Over the past quarter century, my interactions with Brian Little haven’t followed a regular rhythm. Sometimes months go by without any contact. I only see him in person every few years. Yet he’s been there for all my little questions and big milestones.

When I taught my first course, Brian crossed the Canadian border to deliver a guest lecture to my students and got a standing ovation. When I got married, he and his wife made the trip again to celebrate with us. And 15 years after I took his class, when I gave my first TED talk, Brian was in the greenroom helping me manage my nerves…even though he was the next speaker onstage. For me, it was like opening for the Beatles.

Today, Brian is 85, and he still sends me regular pebbles and checks in to see how I’m _really_ doing. Recently, he asked what kind of magic I’m hoping to make this year. I haven’t told him yet, but this book is my answer.

Like the best illusions, a great interaction can surprise us with the joy and awe it sparks. But a vibe doesn’t just appear out of thin air. It’s the magic we make together.
