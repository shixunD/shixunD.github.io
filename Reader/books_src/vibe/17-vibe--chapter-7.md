## Chapter 7

Planned Serendipity

How to Choreograph a Gathering

I am all in favour of spontaneity, providing it is carefully planned.

—John Gielgud

One summer in middle school, I went to a neighborhood barbecue next door. The chitchat wasn’t engaging and there wasn’t anyone my age, so I ended up shooting baskets on my driveway. A few minutes later, I heard a voice challenging me to a game of H-O-R-S-E.

It was a new neighbor named Jeff Zaslow, who was also bored by the BBQ small talk. He’d just moved in across the street with his wife and young daughters. I immediately recognized a fellow nerd—he mostly missed the rim but was full of factoids about my favorite athletes. He asked about my hobbies and lit up when I mentioned magic tricks. Years later, I was flabbergasted to find out that hundreds of people owed their most important bond to him. Jeff was responsible for at least 78 marriages.

He wasn’t your typical matchmaker; he didn’t fix people up on dates. His specialty was hosting gatherings where strangers found love. As a newspaper reporter, Jeff didn’t set out to play Cupid. In his late 20s, _The_ _Wall Street Journal_ assigned him to cover the nationwide search for a new advice columnist to replace the beloved Ann Landers. To get a fresh angle on the story, he entered the contest himself. When an interviewer questioned his qualifications, Jeff retorted: “I may be 28, but I have the wisdom of a 29-year-old.” He got the gig.

Readers loved his wit and candor. When a man sought advice on convincing his girlfriend Julie to get breast implants, Jeff fired back:

Julie deserves someone who loves her for who she is, not how she looks in a sweater. If you can’t do that for her, she won’t need implants anyway because she will already have a big boob in her life. You.

Many readers were looking for love, and Jeff wasn’t content to just dole out tips. He wanted to help. He invited his readers to Zazz Bash, a singles party at a restaurant in Chicago, and donated the proceeds to charity. Over 1,300 people poured in—not just from Illinois but also from Iowa, Minnesota, Indiana, Wisconsin, and even Texas. In a room that big, how could anyone expect to vibe?

People were counting on serendipity. Whenever we walk into a group, we cross our fingers for good fortune. We hope we’ll meet a new friend at a party, have a creative collision at the watercooler, and bump into the love of our lives at the bar. But the truth is that behind the scenes, the best gatherings aren’t the product of random chance. They’re carefully choreographed.

At his singles event, Jeff Zaslow didn’t leave people to their own devices to meet. He spent three months orchestrating structures and rituals to spark connection. To create a range of shared experiences, he arranged live music and dancing along with stand-up comedy, a magician, and a psychic. As an icebreaker, he handed out charts showing how much Elvis would weigh on different planets (97 pounds on Mercury, 648 on Jupiter). To lower the barrier to approaching strangers, he asked everyone to wear nametags and write a favorite word on them.

Jeff worried the nametags would be corny, but they made it easy for guests to connect around common ground. A woman named Laurene was on her way out the door when a guy saw “dancing” on her nametag and convinced her to stay. She ended up falling for his shy friend. Less than a year later, they were the first Zazz Bash couple to get engaged.

The planning paid off. Most of the attendees didn’t find love, but they had so much fun that they were eager to come again the following year—and bring their friends. A few weeks later, Jeff wrote to his readers asking for criticism of the event and suggestions on “non-nerdy activities” to create a warmer vibe. _One lesson: Never serve garlic bread again._

Zazz Bash became an annual event sponsored by Jeff’s employer, the _Chicago Sun-Times_. To spark connections, Jeff introduced games like shoe-matching: Guests met by throwing one of their shoes onto the floor, grabbing one from the pile, and holding it up for the owner to claim. To introduce novel ways to bond, he surveyed his readers in advance on their interests and arranged relevant activities. When _Seinfeld_ was the theme, people bonded through a cereal-eating competition, lookalike contests, and a Kramer dance. Jeff encouraged everyone to have a good time—and to “help someone else have a good time.” They even did that with their exes. At least two couples who’d broken up reconnected at a later Zazz Bash and ended up getting married.

Over the 12 years that Jeff hosted his Zazz Bash, it blossomed into one of the country’s biggest singles parties, drawing over 7,000 people. The bonds went beyond love—people exchanged business cards and made friends too. After two guys, Yomi and Rob, hit it off in the men’s room, Yomi ended up hiring Rob as his lawyer and setting him up with a colleague, who became his wife.

Great gatherings are the foundation of collective connection. Yet they rarely work if they’re haphazardly thrown together. They’re facilitated through deliberate design. A clear vision can coordinate separate individuals into a dazzling ensemble.

### Lighting Up the Room

Gatherings are events where people assemble in groups. They include social events like parties, dinners, hangouts, and club get-togethers, as well as work events like meetings, orientations, huddles, conferences, forums, seminars, and retreats. Even our carpools and kids’ soccer games are gatherings. Yet too many events are poorly conceived. They fill us with dread, drag us down, and drive us apart.

Effective meetups bring us closer through choreography. Skilled hosts arrange a sequence of movements that enable connection. That’s not about social engineering—it’s about creating the conditions in which serendipity is more likely. The interactions aren’t scripted, but the stage is set.

The most basic problem with gatherings is that we’re unclear about the purpose and the plan. _Why are we even here?_ On Saturday you arrive at a party where everyone is too busy watching the game to have conversation. On Sunday you show up for a book club and discover you’re the only one who bothered to read the book. On Monday you head-bob through another pointless meeting that could’ve been an email.

At work, nearly a third of meetings may be unnecessary. For every thousand people in a workplace, that’s $25 million a year of wasted time. _Not accounted for: ideas lost, energy drained, souls crushed._ At one software company, people saved about an hour a week by auditing their calendars to see which meetings were worthwhile—and saved an average of 11 hours a month when teams deleted all recurring meetings and built their schedules from scratch.[[*1]](017_c007_Chapter_7_Planned_Se.xhtml#footnote-033 "footnote") As Amber Naslund, a sales director, advises, “Stop calling meetings so you can simply organize your own thoughts with witnesses.” There are only four reasons to meet: to bond, learn, create, and decide. If we’re not gathering to achieve at least one of those goals, we don’t need to gather.

Once we’ve cut what’s simply unnecessary, we can focus on the purpose and the plan. On the speaking circuit, I kept hearing rave reviews of a gathering guru named Priya Parker, who specializes in renovating cliched celebrations and rejuvenating monotonous meetings. She recommends creating a bold vision that appeals to your ideal attendees and stirring up excitement by announcing an event they’d rearrange their schedules to attend.

In one of Priya’s workshops, a leader was planning a team meeting on “Listening to Customer Feedback.” It sounded boring and passive, so Priya challenged him to signal a punchier vibe. He came back with “Why Do Our Customers Hate Our Product? A Roundtable.” Now it was obvious that they were going to be allies solving a problem together—and they could have a little pal fun along the way.

Great gatherings connect us on common wavelengths. They transport us into a different world as a group, so we find ourselves in a bubble together as pals, allies, or companions. The purpose comes to life when rituals are designed to address shared needs, goals, interests, or values.

One of the best examples I’ve seen is Admin Night. It was conceived by a journalist named Chris Colin, who invites a bunch of friends over to power through their most tedious administrative tasks together. As they pay bills, request refunds, unsubscribe from streaming services, and adjust retirement plans, they entertain each other and exchange advice. A group “getterdone” is more fun than tackling a to-do list alone. Colin reports that “it turns our private drudgery into communal solidarity.”

Steve Martin, Martin Short, and Tom Hanks have their own version of transforming individual discomfort into shared fun: Colonoscopy Parties. They schedule their procedures back-to-back on the same day. The night before, they gather at 5 p.m. to inhale liquids, eat Jell-O, watch comedy, and play poker to determine who has to go last.

When people already know each other, designing rituals around a common purpose is enough to bring them closer together. But in gatherings of strangers or acquaintances, it takes structure to build bridges between them.

### Mixing It Up

At mixers, the purpose is clear: Meet people. Yet they’re rarely designed with enough intentionality and structure to motivate people to mingle. Imagine it’s Friday night at 7 p.m., and you’ve just walked into a cocktail party in New York City. Nearly 100 leaders across a range of fields are milling around, munching on hors d’oeuvres and pizza while drinking wine, beer, or soda. Instead of making new connections, over the next hour, you mostly end up hanging out with your old friends.

This scene is not a hypothetical; it’s an actual study where researchers electronically tagged cocktail party attendees to track their movements. Beforehand, 95 percent of the guests said they were going to network with new contacts. The room was mostly filled with strangers. Yet attendees were twice as likely to talk with people they already knew—and more than three times as likely to talk with those they knew well. They had the longest conversations with their closest friends.

People rarely mix at mixers. At social and professional events, instead of braving the anxiety and awkwardness of approaching someone unfamiliar, we cling to the comfort of chatting with our existing crew. It starts before we even arrive: The fear of walking into a room alone leads us to recruit people to join us. Having a buddy by our side may be soothing, but it stops us from branching out. At the New York cocktail party, the more friends people had in the room, the fewer new ties they made.

This is why social events—not just work meetings—need structure. In rooms of strangers and acquaintances, a little choreography can go a long way toward expanding our circles. You can see it in studies of events that are specifically designed to foster connection.

As nearly a thousand people got ready to attend an IT conference, researchers decided not to leave them to fend for themselves. They randomly assigned some of the attendees to receive an email recommending 16 potential contacts in the room to network with—and prompting them to introduce themselves.

That direction made a big difference in turning strangers into associates and allies, but the impact depended on gender. It didn’t matter much for men: In a room full of tech bros, they had an easy time striking up conversations with new contacts. For women, who were in the minority, it was transformative. Compared to the control group, women who got the list of people to meet ended up connecting with 57 percent more new people—and spent 90 percent more time talking to new people. Five months later, they had 29 percent more LinkedIn connections. And over the next year, those links paid off: Women were 60 percent more likely to get a new job.[[*2]](017_c007_Chapter_7_Planned_Se.xhtml#footnote-032 "footnote")

In a room of thousands, curating connections for each individual sounds like a lot of work. It wasn’t. The attendees didn’t know it, but the 16 recommended contacts were picked at random. To broaden their circles, they just needed to go in with a plan to meet a handful of people…any people. It put some scaffolding around the free-for-all, turning interaction into an assignment. Choreography doesn’t have to be complicated.

Gentle direction on who to meet is part of good design. It also helps to give guidance on how to communicate and even where to sit. A bit of structure can open the door to something deeper than small talk.

### Prompt and Circumstance

Thomas Jefferson wasn’t a big fan of small talk. At his famous dinner parties, he typically had eight to fourteen people sitting at a table, and he facilitated group discussion on philosophical, artistic, and scientific issues. To level the playing field, he ditched rectangular tables and had people sit in a circle, like the Knights of the Round Table.

Jefferson was onto something. In experiments on table shapes, corners activate a desire to stand out while circles shift the focus toward belonging. At a rectangular table, there’s a clear hierarchy. The people at the ends are in the power seats, while those in the middle are stuck turning their heads back and forth. As Larry David said on _Curb Your Enthusiasm_ , not everyone can middle! At a round table, you don’t need to middle. Everyone is an equal participant.

Seating is a form of structure, and it can be designed to expand connection. Many studies show that in classrooms, assigned seats help students widen their spheres. When seating charts are randomized, elementary and middle schoolers are more likely to make new friends. In retirement communities, over 90 percent of senior citizens choose the same seat at meals, confining themselves to a few connections. If tables are rearranged to form T shapes instead of rows, they have more collective conversations. When chairs in the lounge are arranged in groups, seniors interact more between meals—and become happier.

When we chat in pairs, it’s natural to riff. Even conversations about boring topics turn out to be more interesting than we expect, because participation is frequent and dynamic. In groups, people who lack status are often shy about speaking up. Research suggests that to motivate them to participate, all it takes is for the person in charge to make eye contact with them. _They literally feel seen._

Without structure, too many group discussions go off the rails. It’s common for one person to hog the mic, talk over others, and go on tangents that lose the rest of the room. The person dominating and interrupting is likely to be a man—especially if a woman is talking. It’s been documented in a wide range of group discussions, as well as speeches on the Senate floor and oral arguments at the Supreme Court. Due to gender stereotypes that persist despite their injustice, men who run their mouths are seen as confident experts, whereas women who engage in identical behavior tend to be judged as rude and cold. It’s long past time to shatter this double standard, and structure can help. When women are in the minority, requiring unanimous consensus rather than just a majority vote has been shown to balance the conversation. Announcing a round robin where everyone talks also works well. Giving feedback or even displaying a timer can be enough to equalize speaking time and put an end to bloviating.[[*3]](017_c007_Chapter_7_Planned_Se.xhtml#footnote-031 "footnote")

To prevent people from hijacking his dinner parties, Thomas Jefferson set strong norms. He kept conversation focused by prompting people to debate major issues of the day, ranging from the French Revolution and Aaron Burr to the legal system and international diplomacy. He banned chitchat and side conversations. He also forbade people from making toasts and taking partisan positions on politics.

Clear norms don’t just keep people from veering off topic. They also take unwanted rituals off the table, enabling people to vibe on different wavelengths. Priya Parker once coached an exhausted journalist to shake up a ladies’ night by planning the Worn-Out Moms Hootenanny. She only needed one rule: If you talk about your kids, you have to take a shot of tequila. It was a fun consequence that kept the pal energy light.[[*4]](017_c007_Chapter_7_Planned_Se.xhtml#footnote-030 "footnote")

The right norms and rituals can even bring strangers closer together. Between 2010 and 2026, over 20,000 young adults shared some of their deepest emotions with people they’d just met. The goal of The Dinner Party was to give people in their 20s and 30s a place to grieve together. They gathered in small groups for potlucks to share their experiences of loss. The purpose of companionship was clear, and so were the rituals and rules for showing care. All feelings welcome; show up to listen and support; no advising, fixing, or solving allowed.

I happen to love fixing and solving, so my favorite events structure opportunities for strangers and associates to become allies. My go-to activity is the Reciprocity Ring, a helping circle designed by entrepreneur Cheryl Baker and sociologist Wayne Baker. In the original version, people form groups of eight to ten to make requests for something they want or need but can’t get on their own. There’s only one rule: They have to be willing to help others fulfill their requests. I’ve modified it for bigger groups: Put sticky flipcharts around the room, have people attach their asks to the wall, and invite everyone to sign up where they think they can contribute. In my experience, about 80 percent of the requests get meaningful help. Wayne’s data demonstrate that the exercise produces an outpouring of gratitude and establishes a group norm of generosity. Instead of just looking to return the favor, people are eager to pay it forward. As people follow up on the offers, I’ve seen them find their dream job, plan their dream vacation, and meet their dream therapist.

It’s possible to scale the norm of generosity to much larger gatherings. At the Burning Man festival, over 70,000 people gather annually in the Nevada desert to spend a week building a temporary city, making art installations, and partying. The event is designed to move people past associate transactions and toward genuine ally, pal, and companion bonds. Once they’ve bought a ticket and brought what they need to survive, no money is exchanged. Everything else is gifted, ranging from handmade jewelry to homemade food. Careful tests show that after participating in these gifting rituals, people go on to behave more generously toward others—even six months after the event. The collective effervescence of gifting as well as dancing leaves them with a stronger sense of community and a deeper connection to something larger than themselves.

It isn’t only the burst of activities that brings people closer at Burning Man. Throughout the week, people visit a large wooden temple to leave mementos and inscribe messages about loss and trauma. At a ceremony on the final night, they burn the temple as a symbol of catharsis. Bonds are bolstered as people sit together in silence.

### When Silence Is Golden

For a long time in groups, I did everything I could to prevent silence. I saw pauses as a sign that I’d lost the room, so I’d rush to fill the void. Then I came across evidence that although strangers and acquaintances find a lull in conversation awkward, friends aren’t fazed by it. In fact, in many East Asian cultures, silence is a regular feature of group interaction: People are expected to collect their thoughts before jumping in. I’m more accustomed to the American norm of treating conversations like a game of ping-pong—as soon as the ball comes to you, you hit it back. Yet I’ve started to appreciate that pauses can actually be catalysts for connection.

When I had to teach over Zoom during the pandemic, I was worried no one would jump in. _The first law of thermodynamics says that energy cannot be destroyed, but the authors of that law never attended a virtual meeting._ That fall, my classes had the most electric discussions of my career. I’d asked them to type their questions, comments, and challenges in the chat window, and set a norm of stopping every few minutes to review. As we all sat reading quietly together, I realized that group silence can add to the vibe in a gathering.

There’s extensive evidence that if people have quiet time to write down their reflections, groups have more creative chemistry. After all, our first idea is rarely our best idea. At Amazon, at the start of important decision meetings, everyone sits in silence reading a six-page structured memo. When they kick off the conversation, they’re better prepared to disagree thoughtfully and then commit to a common direction.

Silence can help people connect on different wavelengths at social events too. One day the author-artist Suleika Jaouad told me her favorite way to build bonds is hosting journaling clubs. To skip small talk, she kicks off her gatherings by asking participants to reflect and write before they share. She brings people together as pals with a lighthearted prompt, like _Draw a giraffe with your eyes closed._ Hilarity ensues as people show pictures of flying horses with enormous spot-covered teeth. To unite allies, she invites people to write about something they have to offer. _What’s your secret talent?_ People sometimes join forces around shared passions, from providing a dog-finding service to building a wedding photography business. To make way for companionship, she uses more emotional prompts. _What’s on your to-feel list? What’s the self you’ve abandoned that might be worth revisiting?_

I didn’t give journaling clubs much more thought until I went to a retreat with two dozen people—mostly strangers and a handful of acquaintances. The invitation mentioned that it would be intimate, and I assumed it was a reference to the group being much smaller than a typical conference. When the host announced the daily warm-up activity, I froze.

Each morning, we would receive a prompt, spend 15 minutes journaling about a profound personal experience, and then read what we wrote out loud to the whole room. You’d think a psychologist would love this stuff, but I am not that kind of psychologist.

The first morning, the prompt was _Share a time when you followed your intuition._ The idea of talking about my feelings with a bunch of strangers made me queasy, so I played it safe and went with a story I’d told plenty of times. _Whew…one down, two to go._ But other people got real, and I was…moved. One participant talked about how he trusted his instinct to abandon his budding career as a trial lawyer after he vomited before his opening statement. _He took “follow your gut” literally._

The second morning, the prompt was _Write about a time when you bounced back from adversity._ Suddenly the words started pouring out of me. I was surprised to find my eyes welling up as I shared how my wife Allison had overcome childhood medical trauma and rewrote her story as a novel, _I Am the Cage_ , and was now writing a poetic memoir about her experience with breast cancer, _Lump._ In a matter of minutes, I had new companions and allies—one attendee had undergone a similar procedure, and another had already downloaded the novel.

The last morning brought the most revealing prompt. _Draw a picture of your childhood home, circle a room, and share a memory from it._ The stories were remarkable, and I found myself wishing we had more time for them all. One participant chose her father’s library. He’d passed away years earlier, and she still goes there to immerse herself in the scent of his books. An acquaintance of mine circled her attic. A couple had been subletting it, and one day they were gone. Her parents never explained why, and it was only years later that she learned they died of AIDS. Later that day on a hike, she told me that the experience led her to decide that in her future relationships, she would never leave anything important unspoken—even if it meant hurting feelings in the moment. She reached out after the retreat to say that although we’d known each other casually for nearly a decade, she regretted not going deeper sooner. “I like the beginning of our friendship,” she texted.

If we were in pairs, I could have imagined going quickly to deep talk. In a group, though, we needed quiet reflection to prepare something vulnerable that we felt comfortable sharing out loud. Silence was a key feature of the carefully choregraphed ritual. The titrated increase in the intensity of the prompts eased the transition from strangers and associates to potential companions. A year and a half later the whole crew was still sharing pebbles in a group text and meeting up for reunion dinners.

### Taking the Plunge

I’ve learned to gauge the lasting vibe of a gathering by looking for two emotions afterward. One is whether attendees feel FOMO—the fear of missing out on the next one.[[*5]](017_c007_Chapter_7_Planned_Se.xhtml#footnote-029 "footnote") The other is whether people who declined the invitation feel what I call ROMO—the regret of missing out on the last one.

As an introvert, I didn’t expect to like gatherings, let alone feel FOMO or ROMO. Yet I’ve come to love retreats…as long as they’re curated. I spend a few days making connections and then escape back into my cave. Going to the same gathering annually becomes a reunion—a ritual to reconnect with dormant ties.

For too long, past hauntings of shyness and social exclusion stopped me from planning my own retreat. _What if no one wants to come? What if everyone hates it?_ Recently, I decided it was time to face my fear and try to apply the knowledge I’ve gained. I invited about a hundred people from different walks of life to spend a weekend together. Some I knew well; others I was excited to get to know better. We discussed how to build better lives and a better world—topics ranged from raising resilient kids to maintaining our humanity in the age of AI.

When I collected feedback immediately afterward, the overwhelming theme was _too little choreography_. The purpose and plan weren’t clear enough at the outset; people were nervous about approaching strangers. Coordinating group discussions was challenging—many participants chimed in with tangential points rather than advancing the collective conversation. One attendee said it felt like watching toddlers in parallel play.

The following year, I designed a new event. To clarify the purpose and encourage attendees to interact as curious allies and playful pals, I described the event as “nerd camp” for adults. To minimize social anxiety, instead of hiding the guest list, I sent out a bio book in advance. To go beyond professional resumes and promote pal connections, I asked people to list their dream dinner guests as well as their favorite book, movie, or show.

When I noticed that two participants did improv comedy as a hobby, I invited them to lead a workshop on the spot. _It would’ve been ironic if they weren’t willing to improvise._ We all laughed as we pretended the first few people we met were our long-lost twins—and gave minimally offensive insults to the next few. _I once disliked someone with your first name._ It was the ultimate speed dating for new pals—and collaborating to make our partners look good with “yes, and” kicked off an ally vibe. _Yes, my shoelaces are ugly…and so are the bottoms of your shoes!_

The hurdles to connection were high. When strangers interact, the more people they have in common, the more easily they vibe. It’s the transitive property of friendship. If we like the same people (or dislike the same people) we can trust each other. But I’d deliberately recruited people from varied backgrounds. To build more bridges, I set some norms:

  1. Avoid talking to people you already know—and people from your field.

  2. Be a circle opener. If you see someone hovering outside a group, invite them in.

  3. No devices in group sessions or meals. If you need to use a phone, step outside.




When I saw old friends chatting, I chided them with a smile. _You already know each other! Separate immediately!_ To model circle opening, I introduced people with specific reasons to connect on different wavelengths. For pals, I flagged similar interests. _You love crossword puzzles—how are you not hanging out already?_ For potential allies, I highlighted shared goals. _You’re fellow crusaders for critical thinking—you should compare notes._

After catching a few people violating the phone rule, I added a creative punishment. Moving forward, anyone caught scrolling or texting during a session or meal would have to play Russian roulette with Bertie Bott’s jelly beans. If you haven’t tried them, they’re truly disgusting—regular jelly beans are mixed in with foul flavors from _Harry Potter_ , including barf, rotten eggs, boogers, and earwax.

To foster new connections, I assigned people to different seats for each dinner. To choreograph novel conversations, I wrote questions for each round table. _Which fictional character would you elect president? Is the greatest sitcom ever_ Seinfeld _,_ Friends _, or_ The Office _? Adam did the dinner groupings—why do you think he put you together?_

At dessert time, I prompted people to play musical chairs. The next two nights, we progressed toward richer and deeper topics, encouraging people to move toward becoming companions as well as pals and allies. _What’s considered acceptable today that will be seen as morally wrong in the future? Who has it tougher right now: boys in school or women at work? If you were redesigning this gathering, what’s the biggest change that you’d make?_

In the months that followed, a steady stream of emails, calls, and texts poured in from attendees sharing that they’d formed stronger bonds that weekend than anywhere they’d been since college. One wrote that it “wins the world record for me making meaningful connections over a short period to people who could become lifelong friends.” Another noted, “I feel lucky to have made connections that so oddly already feel like lifelong friendships.” Those reflections inspired me to write this chapter—and reminded me of the magic of singles finding love at the Zazz Bash.

* * *

![](assets/page_3_2.jpg)

Looking back, everything I’ve learned about successful gatherings comes back to Jeff Zaslow. He showed me that you don’t have to be a social butterfly to run a great event—you just have to be a thoughtful host, in both senses of the word. He considered every detail in advance, and he was unflinchingly considerate toward countless strangers who opened their hearts to him.

Jeff went out of his way to connect people even when he wasn’t the host. At my wedding, multiple people commented that they loved meeting the witty journalist who opened circles for them. At a birthday party a few years later, Jeff told me that if I ever wanted to write a book about my research on generosity, I should give him a call. In 2011, I took him up on it. He offered sage advice, welcomed me with open arms into the publishing community, and offered consistent encouragement. “Here’s to hoping you’re the next Malcolm Gladwell,” he wrote one day, teasing me. “The little schnitzel across the street is going to be famous!”

In 2012, I was finishing the book when tragedy struck. Jeff died in a car accident. He was heading home from a gathering in northern Michigan. He’d driven four hours all the way upstate, through the snow, to greet readers and sign his latest book.

As I grieved by reading every eulogy I could find, I kept coming back to the joy Jeff took in bringing people together. “Of all the things he did, I think he loved that most of all—it made his day to hear from couples who met at Zazz Bash when they’d had kids,” his wife, Sherry, tells me. “It didn’t happen spontaneously. It took a lot of planning. It was a social event with a purpose, and the purpose was clearly stated: to meet each other and hopefully find love. He would be beaming at all the Zazz Bash babies.” His family estimates that his events brought more than 170 children into the world.

I’ve worked to pay it forward by hosting more gatherings, but I’ve realized that it’s even more important to share his principles and help people broaden and deepen their bonds. Jeff’s philosophy of gatherings was simple but profound. To paraphrase Thomas Jefferson, he understood that when you light another’s candle, it doesn’t darken yours—it can brighten yours too.

“I miss the light Jeff added to the world,” a mutual friend wrote me one day. “But I can see the glow from countless candles he lit.” That’s the purpose of gathering: to light as many candles as we can.
