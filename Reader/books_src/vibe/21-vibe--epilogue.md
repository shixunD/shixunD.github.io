## Epilogue

In the mid-1800s, Nathaniel Hawthorne worried that a new technology would ruin our face-to-face connections. It wasn’t the telegraph—it was the iron stove. He feared that without the warmth of gathering around an open fire, we’d lose our sense of family, friendship, and community. But abandoning the hearth didn’t kill our vibe. It changed where and how we connect.

Many of the most important inventions in human history were made for connection. Writing tools from sticks and papyrus to quills and ink to the printing press allowed us to record our thoughts and share them with others. Transportation vehicles from ships to cars to planes accelerated our ability to cross vast distances to meet new people and reunite with old friends. Communication tools from landlines to smartphones enabled us to interact instantaneously with people around the globe. Computers, the internet, and social media opened the door for billions to exchange information.

These advances have taught us that all solutions introduce new problems. To paraphrase philosopher Paul Virilio, inventing the car also invented the car crash. Inventing the internet and social media also invented cyberbullying, cybertheft, digital surveillance, and digital disinformation. Whether the upsides of new technologies outweigh the downsides doesn’t just depend on how we design them. It hinges on how we adapt to them…especially in the age of artificial intelligence.

AI tools are changing how we interact. A slew of studies show that chatbots can help us build connections. They assist us in communicating our thoughts more clearly, particularly if writing is hard for us. We can turn to them for ideas on planning a surprise party for a pal, an engaging icebreaker for new allies, a thoughtful thank-you gift for an associate who went above and beyond, or a sympathetic message to a companion who’s grieving. They can offer unlimited suggestions for establishing, evolving, resetting, and repairing connections. Conversations with chatbots can shield elderly people from the loneliness of isolation and adolescents from the pain of depression and anxiety. They allow teens to be vulnerable in seeking emotional support—and homework help—around the clock without feeling embarrassed or burdening others.

At the same time, AI has unintended consequences for interaction. When my son Henry was 11, his school started introducing AI tools. One day I asked how he expected them to affect our well-being:

Me: Do you think AI will make people happier or unhappier?

Henry: I think they’ll be less happy, because they don’t have to work for answers.

Me: Does working for answers make you happy?

Henry: It makes me happy when I challenge myself.

If we’re not careful, AI will become a crutch that removes the challenges from our interactions. More and more people are relying on machines to do the hard work of communicating for them. It’s easy to ask a chatbot for advice, but it’s a missed opportunity to reach out to friends, family, and colleagues who could have counseled us. It’s easy to ask an AI agent to draft a script for a first date or a second interview, an email to a frenemy, or a text to an ex, but it deprives us of the chance to figure out how to have hard conversations ourselves.

The more we delegate our interactions to machines, the less equipped we become to deal with uncertainty, complexity, and conflict. “What happens when an entire generation forgets how to hold hard conversations, navigate rejection, or build trust with another human being?” education expert Mandy McLean asks. “We told ourselves AI would give us more time to be human. Offloading dinner reservations might do that, but offloading empathy will not…. We are not just outsourcing cognition, we are teaching a generation to offload connection.”

I’ve sat down with many of the AI pioneers who created tools like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini—including the founders of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind. When I ask about their biggest fears, the most consistent theme is not a far-off doomsday scenario where AI eliminates humans. It’s a foreseeable future where AI eliminates human bonds.

After chatbots burst on the scene in 2022, it took them less than a year to surpass the average person in expressing empathy. Dozens of experiments have already shown that when people receive compassion by text, they feel more supported if it comes from a large language model than a human. Chatbots consistently outperform trained professionals in showing concern—including therapists and crisis counselors, doctors and nurses, and teachers. Busy humans tend to give shorter, less personalized, more judgmental, less caring responses than bots.

Fortunately, when most people find out a message was AI-generated, they no longer prefer it…for now. And to reduce loneliness among college freshmen, daily texts are more effective with a randomly selected peer than a chatbot. We don’t know how long that will last. Critics have sounded the alarm about chatbots becoming sycophantic servants that shower us with praise and validation. When receiving frictionless flattery becomes our dominant interaction ritual, human communication starts to feel like a slap in the face. Rising numbers of young and older adults are forgoing friendships and even romantic relationships for conversations with chatbots. There are growing concerns that much like social media hijacked our attention, AI is hijacking our attachments.

AI companions are imaginary friends. No matter how sophisticated they become, they can’t substitute for real relationships. Chatbots can help us meet our needs, explore our interests, achieve our goals, and clarify our values. But there’s no compatibility, because the interactions are one-sided—it’s impossible to be on the same wavelength with machines. They can’t be associates or allies, because they don’t have needs or goals. They can’t be pals or companions, because they can’t feel joy or care.

Genuine interaction may be the only thing that robots will never replace. Computers can be programmed to simulate a vibe, but they can’t experience it. They’ll never know the guilt of letting a partner down or the grief of unexpressed love after losing a parent. They can’t conceive of the thrill of teaching their kids to ride a bike or the awe of watching them become wise beyond their years.

Henry was right. In the long run, happiness lies in embracing challenges, not avoiding them. Interacting with machines is easy, and it’s only getting easier. Interacting with people is hard, but it doesn’t have to get harder. Friction is a fact of life, and it’s a desirable difficulty—every social stumble is a teachable moment. An awkward conversation is a tutorial on how to do a stop-and-chat. A difficult collaboration is a seminar on building trust. A fizzled friendship is a crash course on staying in touch. A bad breakup is a master class on handling conflict.

Trial and error is how we learn to shape-shift. It’s how we’ve turned some of the people we disliked in the past into our close pals, allies, or companions in the present. What makes those relationships so rewarding is that they didn’t just fall in our laps—or out of our laptops. The most meaningful bonds are earned.

Any interaction can fail. Yet every interaction has the potential to educate, expand, energize, and elevate us. Long live human connection.
