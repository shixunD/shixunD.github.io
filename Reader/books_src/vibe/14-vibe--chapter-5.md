## Chapter 5

It’s Complicated

Maintaining Messy Connections

The opposite of love is not hate, but indifference.

—Wilhelm Stekel

You’re about to participate in a study of stress during public speaking. You’re going to give a few impromptu speeches—you don’t get the topics in advance. After each speech, a friend in the audience will write you a note to tell you how you’re doing. You’ll have a blood pressure cuff around your arm the whole time to monitor your stress.

You’ve just finished your first speech on whether uniforms should be required in schools when your friend passes you a critical note:

I think you can do better.

After the next speech, the message sounds slightly more reassuring:

I would have gone about it differently, but you’re doing fine.

Which note would stress you out more?

You don’t know it, but the psychologists running the experiment predetermined what your friend would write. They randomly assigned some participants to receive critical comments like the first one—and others to get mixed feedback like the second. Surprisingly, their blood pressure didn’t climb after getting negative notes, but it spiked if they got mixed messages.

I’d always assumed that in an interaction, any positivity was helpful. But if the person who cuts you down starts cheering for you, it doesn’t buffer the bad feelings. It amplifies them.

Our most hurtful ties aren’t the purely negative ones—they’re the complicated ones. When we have both positive and negative feelings toward a person, our vibe is ambivalent. Extensive evidence indicates that ambivalent relationships take an even bigger toll on our mental and physical health than bad relationships. In the public speaking experiment, if you had mixed feelings about the friend who came with you, your blood pressure and heart rate were higher before and after they gave feedback—regardless of what they wrote.

Feedback is only the tip of the iceberg. A five-minute conversation with a friend, family member, or colleague who evokes mixed feelings is enough to raise your blood pressure more than one who elicits bad feelings. If your boss is sometimes supportive and sometimes demeaning, you wind up with more health problems and more missed work than if your boss is only demeaning. And the more ambivalent relationships you have in your life, the more depressed you feel—and the more your heart rate and blood pressure climb under stress.

Ambivalent relationships are unpleasant because the vibe is unpredictable. We fret about them beforehand, uncertain about what to expect. _Should I lean in for a hug or brace myself for a brawl?_ We tense up as they start, unclear on what to do. _Should I share a secret or keep it safe?_ We stay on high alert as they unfold, with our fight-or-flight response on a hair trigger. _How dare you judge my cooking?_ We agonize about ambiguous comments, unsure what to make of them and whether to trust the people who make them. _She said nice shirt…did she really like it or was she making fun of me?_

Even if we’re not stuck with ambivalent ties, their positives give us compelling reasons not to abandon them. The in-laws who sometimes make snide comments about your parenting are always available to watch your kids. The mentor who fails to respect your boundaries goes above and beyond to help you get promoted. The buddy who imposes on you to borrow your car happens to be your favorite dinner party guest. The partner who’s nasty when drunk is the picture of caring when sober.

Improving complicated connections is not about raising our tolerance. It’s not about ignoring their vices. It’s about confining their vices and unlocking their virtues.

### On the Fence

Everyone has both pleasant and unpleasant qualities. Psychologists have discovered that when we look at people’s pluses and minuses together, we end up with four possible reactions to them: positive, negative, indifferent, and ambivalent.

Their Top Virtues |  Underwhelming |  Indifferent _Acquaintance or associate_ |  Negative _Enemy_  
Uplifting |  Positive _Pal, ally, or companion_ |  Ambivalent _Frenemy_  
  
We’re usually clear on what to do with indifferent ties. If we have minimal reactions to their pros and their cons, they’re natural acquaintances or associates. We know what to do with positive connections too: If we adore someone’s strengths and don’t mind their weaknesses, they’re ideal pals, allies, or companions. And we try to escape negative relationships. Of course, we don’t always have the luxury of abandoning our most unpleasant relatives, coworkers, and neighbors. We do what we can to minimize our interactions—and not take their actions too personally.

Ironically, we’re often torn about how to handle our ambivalent ties. Compared to enemies, it’s harder to decide what to do with our frenemies. We appreciate their upsides, but we object to their downsides. Contending with mixed feelings is not an occasional challenge. Empirically, we have about as many ambivalent ties as positive ones. And we don’t seem to get better at handling them with experience.

As we interact with people across situations and over time, we inevitably discover that they have flaws that annoy us and if-then tendencies that offend us. When their drawbacks get under our skin, the vibe becomes ambivalent. We develop mixed feelings about pals when they kill our joy, allies when they interfere with our goals, and companions when they violate our values. _This may explain why many peoplesee pets as such good companions (and sometimes even soulmates). Our cats never lack integrity_.

When ambivalence persists, it’s tempting to demote people to distant associates or avoid them altogether. Unfollow, unfriend, and hope they don’t notice. But there are alternatives to drifting apart and cutting our ties that most of us overlook. I learned about them recently from one of my childhood role models.

### Out of Bounds

In 2004, my hometown Detroit Pistons were poised for a run in the NBA playoffs when they made a controversial move. They decided to acquire Rasheed Wallace, the most volatile player in the game. Rasheed was a powerhouse—a big man who could swish three-pointers and block shots. Unfortunately, he was also a loose cannon. He’d already racked up multiple NBA records for misbehavior: He’d been ejected from more games than any other player and broken his own record for the most technical fouls in a season. He’d been suspended by the league for threatening a referee and by his own team for an altercation with a teammate.

As a lifelong Pistons fan, I was ambivalent about Rasheed. So were many insiders in the league—they recognized his strengths but questioned whether his baggage was too heavy. A legendary executive called the Pistons to warn them: “That guy’s gonna f*ck up your team.”

The gamble was the brainchild of Joe Dumars, a former All-Star player who was now the Pistons’ president. Growing up, Joe was my favorite player—when the Pistons won back-to-back championships, he was the good guy on the Bad Boys. He won the inaugural NBA Sportsmanship Award, and it’s now named after him: the Joe Dumars Trophy goes to the one player each year who best “exemplifies the ideals of sportsmanship on the court with ethical behavior, fair play, and integrity.” When he made the trade to bring Rasheed Wallace to Detroit, the explanation seemed obvious: Joe was forgiving to a fault. The poor guy could see the best in players even when they didn’t deserve it. He must have been ignoring Rasheed’s vices.

Four months later, the headline in the news was “Rasheed Left His Problems in Portland.” He’d been booted from five games the previous season, yet as a Piston he didn’t get ejected once. His suspensions fell from seven games to none, and his technical foul rate dropped as well. Rasheed didn’t just avoid trouble—he transformed the team. The Pistons went from winning 61 percent of their games without him to 77 percent with him. I underestimated him, and I underestimated Joe Dumars too.

Going in, Joe knew Rasheed had a long history of eliciting mixed feelings from his coaches and teammates. They loved his unselfish hustle but hated his selfish outbursts. Joe wasn’t oblivious to that ambivalence. He had a strategy for shifting the vibe.

I first met Joe years ago when we were guests on a podcast together, and I couldn’t wait to find out what his secret sauce was. Since then, we’ve become occasional allies in our shared quest to understand how to bring out the best in people. Joe told me Rasheed wasn’t the first difficult player he managed. He started honing his technique during his playing days with a hot-tempered teammate named Dennis Rodman.

Although Dennis was the best rebounder in basketball, he was a ticking time bomb. He was fined and suspended multiple times for headbutting opponents and skipping training camp, practice, and road games. For his coaches and teammates, it was the ultimate ambivalent relationship: _I need you, I hate you._ When I asked what he learned from playing with Dennis, Joe caught me off guard. “You have to be accepting,” Joe said. “He’s just an eccentric person, and so you have to accept that from him.”

I was incredulous. Accept?! How can you possibly accept headbutts and blowing off practice and games? Yet psychologists have found that Joe was onto something. They’ve discovered it in the birthplace of many ambivalent relationships: marriage.

### Accept and Contain

Renowned marriage psychologists Julie and John Gottman find that 69 percent of relationship conflicts revolve around perpetual problems. _I can’t believe you left dirty dishes in the sink…again! I can’t believe you’re overreacting…again!_

For a long time, many therapists concentrated on change. The goal was to help people recognize and eliminate their vices. Yet that’s not always possible. Some traits are too ingrained; some preferences are set in stone. In the past few decades, couples therapy has undergone a revolution. Instead of just demanding change, partners learn to accept some of one another’s shortcomings.

Acceptance is an antidote to ambivalence. In one experiment, when acceptance was added to therapeutic approaches that emphasized change, couples blamed and lashed out at each other less. When couples therapy included a focus on acceptance, across 17 experiments, marital satisfaction and relationship quality improved.

Of course, not all behaviors should be accepted. There’s a big difference between temporary a**holes and certified a**holes, as organizational psychologist Bob Sutton describes them. A temporary a**hole is someone who’s responding poorly to a bad day. A certified a**hole is someone who repeatedly ruins your day. If people consistently treat others like dirt—and rationalize it rather than showing the desire and capacity for change—they’ve crossed the line into abusive territory.

My advice: Don’t feed the hand that bites you.

Acceptance is not about justifying or excusing abuse. It’s about granting others leeway to engage in behaviors that fall within our zone of appropriate action. _It’s okay that you sometimes forget to put the toilet seat down. I won’t judge you for driving slow—you’re cautious._ Accepting others’ limitations doesn’t always reduce or resolve conflict, but it does make forgiveness easier. And it makes them feel better too. Neuroscientists find that feeling understood is enough to light up the reward centers in our brains.

A perpetual problem in my relationships is that I run late…a lot. I have a chronic inability to disengage from a task or conversation until it’s done—and a chronic tendency to pack too much into my days. My wife, Allison, considers tardiness a mortal sin, so she gets upset if I’m even 37 seconds behind our agreed time to leave for a dinner party (even though she’s already built in 37 minutes of buffer time, and we generally dislike dinner parties). Here’s a typical argument between us:

Me: I know you wanted to leave earlier, but flow states are precious.

Allison: You’re assuming that your time is more valuable than other people’s.

Me: No! I love it when people are late—it gives me time to catch up on emails (and an excuse to play word games).

Allison: But other people think it’s rude!

Me: They should think again. Punctuality was invented in an era when people didn’t carry supercomputers in their pockets. If someone didn’t show up, you had nothing to do—and you couldn’t reach them to find out if they were running behind or needed to reschedule. No longer relevant!

Allison: What about when someone is late to a dinner we’re hosting, and the lasagna is either burned or so cold it needs to be reheated?

In 20 years, neither of us has budged on our views, and this dispute could’ve been a real vibe killer. Allison figured out how to prevent that. She tells me she knows I have a lot on my plate, and there are events where no one is affected if we’re a little late, but there are others when our arrival time is important, because people will be waiting for us to eat. I prioritize being on time in those situations, and it makes her less ambivalent about our relationship. _I think/hope._ It works with allies too: My colleagues have low tolerance for tardiness when an audience is waiting but grant a five-minute grace period for small meetings with flexible start times.

In healthy relationships, acceptance is a two-way street. One of Allison’s ongoing challenges is anxiety, and she’s taught me a ritual to help her confine her nerves. When she’s feeling especially stressed, we set aside a dedicated slot for “worry time” to discuss her concerns later.[[*1]](014_c005_Chapter_5_It_s_Compl.xhtml#footnote-024 "footnote") Instead of trying to change one another’s so-called vices, we’ve learned to accept the vices and contain their impact.

Acceptance is about validating others’ preferences. We’re expressing that it’s okay for them to have thoughts and emotions that differ from ours. Containment is about setting our boundaries, so they know what actions we’ll tolerate. We’re conveying what conduct we dislike—and when we will or won’t accept it. As developmental psychologist Aliza Pressman says, “All feelings are welcome, all behaviors are not.”

### Securing the Perimeter

In the 1990s on _SportsCenter_ , when Michael Jordan made a spectacular shot, the hosts loved to declare, “You can’t stop him—you can only hope to contain him!” Jordan said the toughest defender he ever faced was Joe Dumars, and Joe’s strategy was containment. He couldn’t stifle Jordan’s scoring altogether, but he could force him to take more contested shots from more difficult spots. Joe adopted a similar philosophy to avoid ambivalence with Dennis Rodman.

Joe knew he wasn’t going to erase Dennis’s vices, so he worked to contain them. He acknowledged that Dennis had strong reactions and tried to coax him toward better ways of channeling his emotions. Dennis responded well to the acceptance—sometimes he’d look over and wink at Joe. But there was only so much Joe could do as a young player to limit the blast radius of a teammate. Years later at the helm of the Pistons, when Joe acquired another star with a short fuse, he was ready to accept the emotions and contain the damage.

When Rasheed Wallace landed in Detroit, Joe picked him up from the airport. In the car, he told Rasheed he understood he was passionate, and he didn’t expect him to change that. He just expected him to limit when he lost his cool. Joe set a boundary: no ejections, no suspensions.

Joe: Hey, Rasheed, listen. There’s a line we have to draw. You can dance on that line, you can stand on it—just don’t cross it. One technical foul, you’re standing on the line. Two technical fouls, you’ve crossed it. Don’t get thrown out of games and don’t get suspended.

Rasheed: That’s cool, Joe D.

Joe was avoiding a common mistake. When people disappoint us, it’s rarely due to their actions alone. It’s usually because their actions fell short of our expectations. And in many cases, they had no idea, because we didn’t bother to tell them what was acceptable and unacceptable. Not Joe—he made his expectations crystal clear. After establishing that he accepted Rasheed’s personality, he spelled out the situations where a meltdown would be a big problem:

Joe: I know you’re gonna blow up at these officials. I’m not going to overreact to that, Rasheed. In the first three quarters, have at it, man. That being the case, I can’t have you having these techs in crunch time when the game’s on the line. The team can’t have that. Then I have an issue with that.

Rasheed: Oh, I can do that. By the time the first three quarters go by, Joe D, I promise you, I will have said everything I need to say to those referees by then.

Joe: As long as you get it out of your system in the first three quarters, just don’t screw the game up in the fourth quarter.

Rasheed: I understand, Joe D. I got you.

Instead of reacting in the heat of the moment, the boundary allowed Rasheed to exercise proactive self-control. Joe encouraged him to reflect on his rituals in the fourth quarter. Rasheed knew his primary trigger was unfairness, and he needed a better script to handle it. His emotions didn’t have to change, but his behavior did.

When referees called questionable fouls, instead of screaming at them, Rasheed would wait for his opponents to shoot their first free throw. If they missed, Rasheed would shout, _Ball don’t lie!_ It was his way of saying that the basketball gods were restoring justice. When the game was on the line, Joe reminded Rasheed to follow the script. If he started getting distracted, the coaches called plays that required his full attention.

In that holding container, Rasheed helped power the Pistons all the way to the NBA finals. They were clear underdogs against Kobe Bryant and Shaquille O’Neal on the star-studded Lakers. The Pistons had surged to a shocking 2–1 lead when Rasheed started to unravel in game 4. He shot his headband into the stands and yelled at referees. In the third quarter, he got into a shouting and shoving match with a Laker and had to be physically restrained by teammates.

Later in the game, Rasheed’s if-then response was dramatically different. He didn’t get ejected or commit any technical fouls—he dominated the fourth quarter. “Rasheed Keeps His Head,” one headline boasted; another journalist marveled that “one of the NBA’s most volatile players had the cool of a champion.” He led the Pistons with 26 points and 13 rebounds, and they went on to trounce the heavily favored Lakers 4–1. Rasheed was the missing puzzle piece in their victory, and his teammates were no longer ambivalent about him. He’d become their ideal ally.

Expectations don’t carry equal weight in all relationships. It’s easier to motivate people to live up to our standards when they respect and care about us. _Can you please make sure you don’t leave the toilet seat up when we have guests? If we’re taking multiple cars somewhere, can you agree to drive the speed limit?_ It’s also easier to enforce our boundaries when we’re in a position of power or status. It’s much harder to contain the behaviors of our superiors.

Say your boss has a habit of dumping “urgent” tasks on you at the last minute. Research points to a few options for how to respond. One is to explain how it’s in their interests to address your interests. _I know you want our best work, and our team does our worst work when a deadline is sprung on us._ Another is to frame suggestions as questions. _What do you think about announcing deadlines earlier?_ Advocacy can be threatening; inquiry is empowering, particularly if the boss has a big ego. It can also be effective to seek advice. _Our team consistently struggles in crunch time. Do you have any guidance on how to get more advance notice?_

When peer relationships are ambivalent, it’s possible to relegate them to a specific wavelength. That might involve keeping people in the pal zone if they’re fun but unreliable, or the associate zone if they’re unpleasant or unreliable. Years ago, when a work associate invited my family over for dinner, I wasn’t interested. He had a reputation for being brilliant and selfish. The overlap in our interests was minimal at best, and we were at very different life stages—I had three kids at home while his were grown. I gently explained that I was glad to have him as a colleague but overwhelmed with responsibilities as a parent. After he kept pushing, I found a playful way to be more direct: _I’m trying to get better at saying no. Thank you for the practice._ He laughed and said he understood, and we’ve maintained a distant cordial connection for over a decade.

Not all people will respect our boundaries, and not all vices can be easily contained. But managing vices is only half the strategy. It’s also possible to reduce ambivalence by finding and fostering the virtues in others.

### Clash of the Titans

For the 2011 term, no Supreme Court justices disagreed more than Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Antonin Scalia. He was a conservative Catholic who defended originalist interpretations of the Constitution; she was a liberal Jew who believed the Constitution should evolve as a living document. They held contrasting views on guns, gay marriage, abortion, affirmative action, and the death penalty. When the Court was split 5–4, Ginsburg and Scalia were on opposite sides 93 percent of the time.

Before serving on the Supreme Court together, the two judges had been close friends. They first vibed as pals in the early 1980s, when they both served on the U.S. Court of Appeals as judges for the D.C. Circuit. They bonded around their love of the opera and discovered that they made one another laugh. Their pal connection deepened as their spouses hit it off: They both loved to cook. It became a tradition for the Ginsburg and Scalia families to celebrate New Year’s together. They even took vacations together—on one trip to India, they rode an elephant together. “That was a rather bumpy ride,” Ginsburg said. She could’ve said the same thing about their relationship.

For a while, the two judges avoided ambivalence by sticking to the pal zone and steering clear of politics. When Ginsburg joined Scalia on the Supreme Court in 1993, that was no longer an option. They clashed on nearly every issue, and there was no way to reconcile their differences. With Scalia sitting right next to her, Ginsburg once admitted: “I love him, but sometimes I’d like to strangle him.” It seemed like a textbook ambivalent relationship. Remarkably, though, their ideological divide didn’t interfere with their bond. There’s a study that explains how.

Young adults answered a series of questions about their partners’ faults. A year later, the researchers followed up to see if the couples were still together. Surprisingly, people who saw more severe flaws in their partners weren’t less likely to stick with them. It came down to something else: whether they managed to maintain high opinions of their partners _despite_ their flaws.

People used two different techniques for staying positive about their partners. One was emphasizing their partners’ virtues. _She’s the person I want to talk to before I go to sleep at night._ The other was finding virtues in their vices. _The fact that she’s so blunt means I never have to wonder how she really feels_.

These strategies aren’t only relevant in romantic relationships. Seeing the good in others is not about denying their shortcomings—it’s about appreciating their strengths. You’re not ignoring their bad qualities; you’re taking them in context of their best qualities. That’s what happened with Scalia and Ginsburg.

In the late 1970s, Ginsburg heard Scalia speak for the first time at a conference. Although she objected to the content of his presentation, she downplayed that reaction: “I disagreed with most of what he said, but I loved the way he said it.” She emphasized his many strengths, expressing admiration for his knowledge, wit, and charm. Many years later when they were on the Supreme Court together, Scalia did the same. He accepted their differences and accentuated her strengths:

Scalia: She’s a very nice person. What’s not to like? Except her views on the law….

We agree on a whole lot of stuff. We do. Ruth is really bad only on the knee-jerk stuff. She’s a really good textualist…. She’s obviously very smart.

Both justices appreciated that distasteful beliefs can come from worthy principles. They managed to find a master virtue in the very vices they found most objectionable in one another.

One June morning in 1996, Ginsburg was walking out the door for a trip when Scalia showed up with a stack of papers. It was the near-final draft of his dissenting opinion on a case, and he was delivering it to her in advance. “I want to give you as much time as I can to answer it,” he said. He was conveying that although he vehemently disagreed with her, he still cared about her.

Scalia’s dissent “was a zinger,” Ginsburg said. “I was glad to have the extra days to adjust the Court’s opinion. My final draft was much improved thanks to Justice Scalia’s searing criticism.” Although they had opposing ideological goals, they recognized a compatible professional goal of growth. It prevented ambivalence by uniting them as allies. As Scalia put it, “Ruth and I had developed something of a mutual improvement society.”[[*2]](014_c005_Chapter_5_It_s_Compl.xhtml#footnote-023 "footnote")

It’s probably not a coincidence that Ginsburg and Scalia kindled their pal vibe first. Psychologists find that when building a relationship, it’s easier to start with similarities than differences. If people are on opposing sides first and are then asked to collaborate, they often slip into a nasty routine of cutthroat cooperation. They might pretend to be supportive and stab one another in the back. If people identify a common interest first, they’re more likely to develop a healthy pattern of friendly competition.[[*3]](014_c005_Chapter_5_It_s_Compl.xhtml#footnote-022 "footnote")

The two justices knew from their early connection that they shared some foundational principles. After Scalia died, Ginsburg gave a eulogy. “He was a jurist of captivating brilliance and wit…his pungent opinions so clearly stated that his words never slipped from the reader’s grasp,” she quipped. She honored their connection by quoting from an opera about their relationship: “Different in our interpretation of written texts, one in our reverence for the Constitution and the institution we serve.”

This is the ultimate test of whether an ambivalent relationship is salvageable—and worth saving. At the end of the day, does the person exemplify or violate your most important values?

When he was vetting Rasheed Wallace, Joe Dumars asked himself that question. Joe’s core value was unselfishness; he wanted players who used their skills to elevate the team. For several seasons, Joe had watched Rasheed put his team’s success above his own. He’d given up his starting spot on a roster without complaining. His teammates said he might’ve been _too_ unselfish—he routinely dove for loose balls and passed the ball when he could’ve kept it for himself and padded his stats. Joe recognized a virtue in his vice. “To be a contender, you need a guy who’s a live wire,” he told me:

Before I acquired Rasheed, everything was predictable about my team. I needed a fire-starter. I played with one, I traded for one, and won championships with both of them, and I see how valuable those guys are. Dennis would start the fire every night. Rasheed would start the fire every night. They would not only start the fire—it’s when they were at their best. I knew they weren’t ever going to start a fire to burn the team down.

We can find redeeming qualities in many people. The test of whether a relationship is worth continuing is not whether people have vices. It’s whether their worst vices can be contained or harnessed as virtues.

It’s up to us to decide where our boundaries lie. What’s bearable and unbearable. Where we can bend and how far. When people refuse to respect those boundaries, they start to detract from our lives. They drain our energy, deter our goals, and diminish our ideals.

The connections to maintain are net positive forces in our lives. They enhance our well-being, enable our growth, and enrich our values. I feel no ambivalence about that.
