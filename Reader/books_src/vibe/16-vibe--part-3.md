# Part 3

Scaling Connection

The Roots of Group Solidarity

When our third child was on the way, friends warned us that the biggest hurdle would be zone defense. We soon learned that the real issue wasn’t being outnumbered; it was that the number of arguments between our kids tripled. With two kids, there was only one sibling relationship to manage. With a third kid, all three pairs could be fighting at the same time. As my daughter Elena said when she was nine, “I have a brother…a sister…and a pet. That’s a _lot_.”

We weren’t alone. In many settings, going from a duo to a trio can be enough to cause chaos. Compared to dyads, in triads people get less airtime and are more reluctant to share secrets with potential pals and companions. Whereas pairs can’t gang up on each other or gossip behind each other’s backs, when a third person joins, factions can form and kill the vibe. In a study of Harvard freshmen who were randomly assigned to be roommates, 45 percent of doubles decided to stay together sophomore year, but only 29 percent of triples stuck it out. And yes, there’s some evidence that on average, sibling duos are closer than trios. Two’s company, but three’s a crowd.

Groups don’t just frustrate friends and families; they trouble teammates too. Trios of allies and associates are less satisfied, efficient, and cohesive than duos. It’s true across all kinds of projects—writing together, making decisions together, changing a tire together, and solving big problems together. In a massive analysis of 65 million patent, software development, and science publications, triads of innovators were less likely than dyads to generate major breakthroughs. _When Sartre wrote, “Hell is—other people!” he must’ve meant at least two other people._

The challenge isn’t just going from two to three. The bigger the group, the bigger the problems. Adding a fourth member further reduces satisfaction, motivation, and creativity in teams—and cohesion among roommates. As group size continues to grow, so do coordination and motivation challenges. People struggle to get in sync and feel less personally responsible for the result.

Bonding within a group can be as tough as bonding as a group. We get lost in the crowd, unsure about who to approach or what to say. We fear that if we put ourselves out there, we’ll be judged or rejected. In the words of philosopher Hannah Arendt, “Loneliness shows itself most sharply in company with others.”

The trials and tribulations are only half the story of grouping, though. Group bonds are fundamental to being human—and they’re an essential part of our lives. Sociologists have long highlighted that even when we have individual companions, we can still feel isolated without community. Over and above our individual ties with friends, relatives, and colleagues, belonging to a group plays a central role in our well-being.

In one experiment, adolescents and adults struggling with mental health were randomly assigned to spend six hours coming up with ways to strengthen their sense of group belonging. A year later they were significantly less lonely and depressed—and their well-being improved at least as much as if they’d undergone cognitive behavioral therapy. That’s a remarkable effect given that all they did was make plans to join new groups and get more involved in the existing groups that were important to them.

The more groups we belong to, the better off we are. The more different groups kids and adults identify with, the higher their self-esteem and the better their mental health. Participating in a wide range of social groups—a book club, a sports league, a religious community, a neighborhood association, a school board—predicts gains in happiness and declines in depression over the next few years. At first I thought this was because people who join lots of groups (extraverts) are also the kinds of people who experience rushes of exuberance. But the evidence is clear that even introverts get a happiness boost from participating in groups and going to parties.

What makes groups unique is their ability to offer a sense of belonging and meaning. More than individual relationships, they link us to something larger than ourselves. The founding father of sociology, Émile Durkheim, coined a term for the peak experience of being in a group: collective effervescence. It’s the energy and harmony we find in group interaction rituals with a shared purpose. It’s the electricity we feel when we cheer for our favorite sports team in a stadium—and when we slide into synchrony with associates in a chorus, march alongside allies at a rally, pray with companions at a religious service, or flow with pals during a walk, run, or yoga or karate class. It’s what millions of fans felt on the Eras Tour, wristbands flashing, as they shook it off together.

Collective effervescence is powerful because it fills us with joy and hope. That’s the conclusion of 50 studies spanning more than 180,000 people. When we’re in rhythm together, endorphins and oxytocin flood our brains, our heart rates synchronize, and our sense of self melts into a feeling of oneness with the group, as I once learned accidentally.

I’d agreed to speak at Strive, a weeklong endurance event for charity. When the host mentioned that we’d be biking, hiking, and swimming, I figured I’d be fine—I work out six days a week and a guy twice my age was doing it. I didn’t realize the event would culminate in a 70 km bike ride up a mountain, and I arrived to discover I was the only one who hadn’t trained or brought toe clips. The night before, I could hardly sleep. _What was I thinking? I’m not going to make it._ To my surprise, I ended up riding in harmony with a group of four. As we pedaled for seven hours, hearing them cheering kept me going when I was tempted to quit, and the euphoria of crossing the finish line together solidified our bond. We’d been strangers just a few days earlier, but we quickly became closer pals and allies than many people I’d known for decades. As cohost Noah Devereux told us, “Growth happens when you step outside your comfort zone. Magic happens when you do it with others.”

A strong group vibe makes us feel valued. Vibing with multiple groups offers more wavelengths for connection and more avenues for contribution. And the web of connections we build in groups brings stronger support networks than individual ties can. Research shows that when we belong to clubs and religious communities, we gain more acquaintances and associates who can help us when we need it. And when our people are connected to each other, they can look out for us together. In a cohesive group of friends, it’s easier to count on help from allies and support from companions.

Yet solidarity is an endangered species in the U.S. Historically, women and girls tended to bond in pairs, whereas men and boys favored groups. Now they’re all increasingly opting for solo activities like gaming alone. Over the past few decades, groups have become increasingly small parts of American life. The trend was first documented in _Bowling Alone_ , and it’s not just club sports that have vanished. Since 2000, about 40 million Americans have stopped going to church. Over several decades, membership in parent-teacher associations has plummeted. And in my home city of Philly, block parties are in “mad decline”—there were three times as many in 2008 as in 2023. As writer Josh Lora observes, “People are desperately lonely while also saying their favorite hobby is…staying home.”

Our social circles used to look like interwoven webs. Now they look more like bicycle wheels with a hub and spokes—many of the people in our lives don’t know each other.

Most Americans don’t have a friend group. It’s no surprise that over two thirds of people report missing out on daily collective effervescence and nearly a quarter fail to find it even once a week. The supply of shared joy runs especially short in the U.S. because we tend to see happiness as an individual pursuit, which appears to be a risk factor for loneliness. In East Asian cultures, where people typically view joy as a collective experience, they’re more likely to find happiness when they seek it.

Collective effervescence doesn’t just enhance our well-being. Groups serve a vital role in the social order: They outlast pair bonds. “Damage to a single relationship is enough to make a dyad dissolve,” psychologist Richard Moreland reminds us. “If only one relationship in a group is damaged, then the group can still survive.”

When groups are vibrant, individuals and societies thrive. Yet many of the strategies for building solidarity aren’t intuitive. You’ll see that it’s possible to unite communities by dividing them and build great teams of people who don’t get along. And that the most serendipitous connections can be carefully planned—and the most durable traditions are the most flexible.

We need our groups back. They bring joie de vivre and strengthen our social institutions. One of my mentors, Richard Hackman, pointed out that groups are like amplifiers: whatever you put in comes out louder. To build better collective vibes, we need to improve the signals in our gatherings, communities, collaborations, and families.
